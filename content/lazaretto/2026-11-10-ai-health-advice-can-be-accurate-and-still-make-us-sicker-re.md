Bulletin. STAT News, published November 10, 2026.

The piece, an opinion essay, raises a caution about artificial intelligence systems that dispense health advice. Its central claim, quoted directly: "Even a factually accurate health message can change behavior in ways that make people less healthy."

The argument separates two properties usually treated as one. Accuracy, the correctness of a statement against known medical fact, is distinct from the behavioral effect that statement produces once delivered to a person seeking guidance. A message can satisfy the first standard while failing the second. The authors frame this as a problem specifically for AI-generated advice, where systems are commonly evaluated and benchmarked on factual correctness alone.

The stated concern is that this benchmarking gap matters more as AI tools take on a larger role as medical advisors. If evaluation regimes measure only whether an output is true, they may miss downstream effects on how recipients act, effects that can run counter to health rather than support it.

The essay does not itemize specific failure cases in the material provided here; it argues at the level of principle, that correctness and behavioral safety are separate axes requiring separate scrutiny, and that current assessment practice conflates them.
