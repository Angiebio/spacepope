Researchers report that a brain-computer interface has helped restore both movement and the sensation of touch in a 48-year-old man with quadriplegia, according to STAT News. The system, described as a "double neural bypass," works by routing signals around the site of spinal injury, reconnecting the brain's motor commands to the body and returning sensory feedback in the other direction.

STAT News reports the case as a milestone in brain-computer interface research, noting that most prior devices in this field have restored either movement or sensation, but rarely both in the same patient. Researchers involved in the work say the bidirectional approach, sending signals out to the limbs and back in from them, more closely mimics the brain's natural feedback loops than earlier one-way systems.

The report states that the implant allowed the man to perform physical tasks with restored sensory input guiding his movements, rather than relying on vision alone to compensate for the loss of feeling. STAT News did not specify how long the results have been sustained or how many additional patients have received the same implant.

The work sits within an active field of neural engineering research aimed at bridging damaged spinal pathways using direct brain interfaces. STAT News notes that further study will be needed to determine how broadly the approach could apply to other patients with similar injuries.
