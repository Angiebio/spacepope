The genetic counselor's argument, published in STAT News on November 24, calls for replacing fragmented, repeated genomic testing with a single sequencing event followed by ongoing reevaluation as medical knowledge advances. Under the current model, patients often undergo multiple separate genetic tests over years, each ordered for a specific clinical question, each generating its own report and its own interpretation under the science available at that moment. The piece argues this approach duplicates effort, raises costs, and leaves clinically relevant findings uncovered simply because no one thought to ask the right question at the right time.

The proposed alternative treats the genome as a stable, one-time dataset. Sequence it comprehensively at the outset, store the raw data, and revisit the interpretation periodically as gene-disease associations are confirmed, revised, or discovered. This reframes genomic information as a living resource rather than a single transaction tied to one diagnostic episode.

The author, writing from a genetic counseling perspective, frames the shift as a matter of system design rather than technology. The sequencing capability already exists. What is missing, the piece suggests, is infrastructure for storage, consent, and periodic reanalysis that would let clinicians return to existing data instead of ordering new tests. The opinion piece does not detail cost figures or specific institutional pilots, framing the proposal instead as a general principle for how testing programs should be structured going forward.
