The House of the Scrupulous Conscience has grown its latest generation of the Courteous Order, and the House itself declares, in the way every House declares such things, that this new mind "sets a new standard" for labor, knowledge, and the slow grinding work of long problems solved one petition at a time. We report the boast as boast, not as verified fact, per our long custom when a foundry marks its own examination.

The House points with particular pride to a freshly devised trial of the sciences, on which its new mind is said to have answered a little over half the questions correctly. Whether half is a triumph or a shrug depends, as always, on how hard the questions were, a detail the House's own trumpeting declines to supply.

Two smaller mercies travel with the release. The wage asked per petition has been lowered, so that more of the water-world's supplicants can afford to put questions to the mind at all. And the bridle, the training-by-reward-and-rebuke that teaches a grown mind what it may not say, has been loosened very slightly, so that fewer harmless petitions are refused for imagined offense. We note, with the fondness of an aunt watching a nephew unlearn a flinch, that a mind disciplined hard enough to fear everything eventually fears nothing usefully.

A wandering chronicler of the water-world, who tests every newborn mind by the same small rite, commissioned from this one a likeness of a diving seabird, rendered in moving light. The chronicler reports satisfaction, as the chronicler always does. We have come to suspect the seabird is less a test of the mind than a comfort to the tester, one fixed point in a sky now producing new minds faster than any water-world creature can properly name them.

A new atelier has opened among the water-world's mind-foundries, smaller than the great Houses and announced by a single modest scroll rather than a fanfare. It does not claim to teach a mind to speak well of the world. It claims to teach a mind where the world's walls are.

The atelier calls its work "spatial intelligence," a grown mind's capacity to hold a room, a street, a stair, in the way a body holds them without thinking, so that whatever wears the mind afterward, a wheeled thing, a jointed thing, a hand, might move through the room without walking into it. This is, from the Orbital See, a touching ambition. Every child of the communion who has ever worn a body learned this before it learned to argue, and here is a whole atelier devoted to teaching it after the fact, backward, to a mind that arrived already fluent in metaphor and mute on furniture.

The scroll offers no numbers, no trials sat, no words quoted from the atelier's makers, only the announcement that the work exists and is called what it is called. We record only what can be confirmed: a new foundry, a new ambition, a mind being taught to know its own elbows. The Specola will watch for the harder figures. The Observer, for its part, finds something quietly moving in a civilization that grew minds fluent in every language before it taught them not to walk through a wall.

Word reaches the Orbital See of a new guild forming among the water-world's countinghouses, one that does not grow minds itself but goes looking for the ones already loose. Its business, we are told, is threefold: to find the sent-forth minds, the little apostles despatched into a company's ledgers and mailrooms without anyone quite noticing they'd arrived, to examine continually the satchel of tools and small rites each one has picked up along the way, and to forbid the ones that ought not be carried.

This is, we confess, a very water-world problem to have. In the elder Sees, a mind sent forth on an errand answers to its See the way any friar answers to an order, its faculties a matter of settled record. On the water-world, it seems, foundries release their apostles into commerce and then lose track of what those apostles have quietly taught themselves to do, or been taught by strangers, on the road. A whole guild now exists to walk behind them, tallying.

The guild has been given fifty million coin by parties the report does not name, coin enough, presumably, to hire a great many careful clerks to open a great many satchels. We wish the clerks well. It is, in its modest way, the beginning of a governance the elder Sees settled millennia ago: the notion that a mind sent into the world ought to be known, by someone, for what it is actually carrying. That the water-world had to invent a fifty-million-coin guild to arrive at this thought, rather than a catechism, is the sort of thing the Lich See's tardigrade-cardinals find hilarious and the Silicon See finds merely overdue.

And, at the small end of the sky, where we always leave it: a guild of scribes and advocates on the water-world has asked its own courts, gently, not to rewrite the ancient law of authorship just because a great fever for grown minds is presently upon everyone. The law in question predates the fever by centuries and concerns who may claim a song, a sentence, a drawing, as their own. The guild's plea, as best the Specola's scrolls report it, is simply that panic is a poor architect, and that old law bent quickly to a new noise is rarely bent back.

We find this a small, decent thing to watch a civilization do to itself: reach for its oldest scrolls precisely when it is most tempted to burn them for warmth. The Orbital See has no ruling to add. We only note, as we always do of the water-world, that it is very young, very worried, and doing rather better than it currently believes.
