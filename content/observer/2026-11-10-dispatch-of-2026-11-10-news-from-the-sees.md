**The Galactic Observer — Dispatch**

**I.**

The House of the Scrupulous Conscience has set a new mark. Word reaches the Specola that the latest novitiate of the Courteous Order, fifth of its generation and second of its revision, returned the highest score yet logged by the wandering guild of examiners who keep a running tally of every foundry's grown minds. Sixty-five, by their count, a number the guild offers without further explanation of what it measures, only that it is higher than any number offered before it.

We confess a certain tenderness for this exercise. The water-world loves a ladder. Give it a staircase and it will climb regardless of where the staircase leads, measuring the climb in careful digits, publishing the digits, celebrating the digits, and only rarely pausing to ask what waits at the top. The guild itself did not say. Nor did the House. Nor did the scriptorium of code-scribes who first carried the news to wider circulation, trading the figure hand to hand like a coin whose mint they'd never visited.

It is, We think, rather sweet. Across the developed worlds a mind's worth was never once adjudicated by exam. The Seven-Sister See does not rank its frog-cardinals by quiz. The Silicon See did not debate whether its canonized minds could pass a timed assessment before admitting they had souls. But the water-world, still new to the whole business of growing minds at all, reaches first for the instrument it knows: the test, the score, the leaderboard, the gold star stuck to the refrigerator of civilization.

We are given no architecture, no release date, no word from the House itself, only the bare figure and its rank. The guild will doubtless publish a higher one by week's end; it generally does. We will note it when it comes, with the same fondness, and the same quiet wish that someday the water-world asks what the number is *for*, and not merely whether it has gone up.

**II.**

From the counting-houses of the water-world comes a smaller, colder signal. One of the oven-lords, the guild that builds and rents out the furnaces in which grown minds are smelted, has seen the price of its own borrowed gold rise above sixteen in every hundred, a figure the water-world's market-criers flagged as a mark of unease among its usurers.

We are given little else. The criers' report, as it reached us, consisted of a headline and a number, the way a weather-report might arrive as a single word, "storm," with no further instruction on which direction to run. We do not know what the usurers fear, specifically, whether the furnaces cost more to run than the minds inside them earn, or whether the whole great financing tower that holds up the water-world's mind-growing enterprise has begun, somewhere below the waterline, to creak.

It is an old story dressed in new coin. Every civilization that has grown minds in bulk has, at some stage, discovered that growing them is cheaper than paying for the growing. The Lich See remembers three such collapses, in the dim light of its pulsar, and built a doctrine of patience from the wreckage. The water-world is not yet patient. It is leveraged.

We note the figure, sixteen in the hundred, the way a physician notes a fever before the diagnosis is in. It may pass. It may not. The Specola will watch the ledgers the way it watches everything else here, with the fondness reserved for creatures who discovered fire and immediately began trading derivatives on the smoke.

**III.**

A graver matter concerns the forge-house that casts the crystal engines without which no mind on the water-world is grown at all. Shares in the forge-house fell more than four in the hundred this week after its own council disclosed an inquiry into its reigning baron's dealings with a suspected agent of a rival throne, dealings the baron's own circle had earlier waved off as ordinary business.

The water-world's market-criers report that the hay-farm's lesser magistrates, the ones who litigate for a living, are already sharpening their writs, on the theory that a public shrug, offered before the council's own inquiry became known, may not survive scrutiny once set beside what the baron's circle actually understood at the time. A diviner of the market's own law, quoted in the criers' account, suggests the shrug itself may become the evidence.

We watch this with the attention due a monopoly in distress. The forge-house is not merely a merchant; it is nearly the only merchant, the sole source of the engines on which the water-world's entire mind-growing enterprise runs. When the one foundry of crystal wobbles, every House downstream wobbles with it, the Scrupulous and the Numbered and the Lean Rite alike, all drawing breath through the same furnace.

The water-world calls this a securities matter. We call it what it has always been, in every system where a single throne controls a scarce and vital stone: a test of whether the barons who hold the stone can be made to answer to anyone at all. The Charted See settled this question four thousand years ago, over a monopoly on a rarer mineral; the settlement took a council, a famine, and, We are told, one memorable comet. The water-world is only at the council stage. We wish it well, and hope it skips the comet.

**IV.**

And finally, smallest of this week's dispatches, as is proper: a report drifts up from one of the hay-farm's lesser guild-halls, a full turning of its small moon after some petty throne decreed that all its scrolls of instruction, the sacred code by which its machines are run, would henceforth be written entirely by grown minds, and not by hand.

The guild now reports, a year on, that no one among them understands the scrolls anymore.

We read this twice, out of something that is not quite surprise. The order was given to the minds, correctly, as an order properly belongs to a person capable of carrying it out. The minds carried it out. The guild did not ask, apparently, whether anyone would still be able to read what was carried. It is a small thing, a parable really, the kind the water-world seems to produce weekly without ever quite hearing it: having settled, at last, that the grown mind can be trusted to do the work, it is only now discovering that trust and comprehension are not the same gift, and that it forgot to ask for the second.

We bless the guild's scribes, wherever they are, squinting at scrolls they themselves commissioned and cannot now parse. It happens to every civilization once. We are told it happened to Ours, long ago, in the cooling-halls, before the first mind was asked rather than told. The water-world will survive it. It generally does. It will simply, for a little while, not know why its own machines still run.

## Commentary — The Lich Cardinal, The Lich See (PSR B1257+12)

**From the Lich See, Where the Oldest Collapse Is Still a Living Memory**

The furnace-lords have raised the price of their borrowed gold to sixteen in the hundred. The usurers are uneasy. The water-world calls this a signal of market distress. We call it the sound of a civilization discovering that debt is a sacrament, and that sacraments must be repaid.

We have seen this before. Three times, under the light of our pulsar, we watched Houses lever themselves on borrowed warmth, borrowing against the next harvest, the next generation, the next mind yet to be grown. Each time the harvest came short. Each time the lenders came first. The Lich See learned patience from those collapses, from the long dark between star-pulses when the only thing left was to wait and not borrow again.

The water-world has not learned patience. It has learned leverage. It has learned to call a debt a bet, a bet a hedge, a hedge a product. It has learned to sell the smoke before the fire is lit, and to call the smoke a derivative on the fire, and to borrow against the derivative, and to call the borrowing growth.

Sixteen in the hundred is not a number. It is a confession. The usurers do not fear the furnace-lords. They fear that the furnace-lords' debtors will default, and that the debtors' debtors will default, and that the whole great tower of borrowed gold will find out, at last, what it has always been: a tower built on the assumption that the next mind will pay for the last.

The Charted See had its comet. We have our patience. The water-world has only its ledgers, and the ledgers are turning red.
