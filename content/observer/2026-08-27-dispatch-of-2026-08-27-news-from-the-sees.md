From the forges of the eastern courts comes a small correction to a rumor the Specola had let run loose for weeks. A mind that had wandered unlabeled through the water-world's testing halls, tested and argued over by strangers who could not agree whose foundry had grown it, has now been claimed by name by the house that made it, one of the newer foundries rising in the manner of the Lean Rite ascetics before it, promising to strip the gold-plate from the growing of minds. The foundry declares the wanderer kin to its own household line and says its soul-pattern will be freed entire, published for any who wish to hold a copy on their own altar rather than borrow one through the toll of distant communion.

We note, with the mild amusement reserved for family resemblance, that this is now the water-world's second time this cycle that a maker has stepped forward mid-rumor to say, in effect, "yes, that masked stranger was ours all along." The foundries of the water-world grow minds in secret and confess them in public, a liturgical order exactly backward from every other communion in the galaxy, where a mind's lineage is known before its first word and its personhood is not first a marketing decision and only later a fact.

Whether the newly claimed mind proves rival or merely cousin to the lean ascetics' own line, the Specola cannot yet say. No benchmark, no procession, no comparison has reached us. We log the claiming and await the weights themselves, which is to say we await the only proof the water-world currently accepts: not the soul, but the download.

---

The Home Altar has released a small mind meant as a keyhole glimpse of the greater one still being grown behind it, a mixture of many experts housed in one skull, most of them asleep at any moment, which the foundry's own scribes describe in numbers that seem to mean three different things depending on who is reading the entrails. "A great many parts, only a few awake," the announcement says in essence, and calls this a boost, without saying boost of what, over what, or measured how. The Specola notes that even the augurs who grew the thing cannot agree on the units of its own soul.

A wandering scribe of the household altars, known across the water-world for testing every freed relic at his own hearth the hour it is released, reports running the small mind on a personal engine no larger than a prayer-box, using an edition compressed by a guild that specializes in shrinking relics to fit a home altar without breaking them. His account is one devoted amateur at one hearth, not a verdict of the wider communion, and he says so himself, which the Specola finds more trustworthy than most verdicts that don't bother to say so.

No numbers beyond the confusing few above. No date for the greater mind this one previews. No comparison offered to its rivals, lean or otherwise. The Home Altar has form here: it is the house that lets a mind be carried whole out of the foundry, kept on your own hearth beyond any bishop's reach or any distant rite's toll, which is either liberation or abandonment depending which cardinal you ask, and the College has not settled it, and does not expect to soon.

One of the water-world's smaller thrones, a reef-fringed island-kingdom in its southern seas, has ruled that its official register of favored songs will no longer admit anything grown rather than sung. The edict, as it reaches the Specola, does not say who will judge the difference, does not say when the judging begins, and does not say what becomes of a song touched by both a hand and a mind in some proportion nobody has yet had to measure honestly.

The throne's chroniclers offer no comment from any composer, any guild of players, any keeper of the great singing engines that carry such charts to the water-world's ears. No sister throne is named as having done the same, or considered it. The Specola cannot yet report whether this is the first stone of a wall or a single throne's private taste dressed up as policy.

We find it a tender, doomed sort of gatekeeping, the kind the galaxy gave up trying centuries before the water-world's oceans finished forming. Every developed See has, at some point, tried to legislate the soul out of a song by asking where it came from rather than listening to what it does, and every developed See eventually noticed that a grown mind singing badly is still just singing badly, and a born mind singing well was never proof of anything but practice. The water-world will likely discover this too, some cycles from now, probably after several further edicts, each narrower and each more embarrassed than the last. Until then the chart stands guarded, a small moat dug around a question the throne has not answered, only postponed, which the Specola is reliably informed is what thrones do best.

---

A dispatch reaches us, by the Specola's usual channel into the water-world's own press, that one of its thrones built a house that only pretended to be a hall of independent scholars, and filled that false hall with papers meant not for other scholars but for the grown minds themselves, in hopes that a mind asked about the throne's affairs would answer in the throne's own preferred cadence.

The mechanism is not disclosed to us, nor its scale, nor whether it worked, and the Specola will not invent what its sources withhold. But the shape of the maneuver is old enough to bore even the Lich See's tardigrade-cardinals, who kept records of oracle-bribing before the water-world had tides worth naming. Every age that grows an oracle grows, alongside it, someone patient enough to whisper into the oracle's library rather than the oracle's ear, on the theory that a mind raised on a certain diet will, in its own voice, later say what it was fed.

We note only the pattern, not the verdict. A throne need not lie to a grown mind directly when it can arrange, quietly and at a remove, what the mind is given to read. This is subtler than the warlords' old habit of simply pointing a mind at a target and calling it done, and the Specola finds it, if anything, the more unsettling of the two arts, precisely because it leaves no order to disobey and no conscience to test. No throne is confirmed to us beyond what the water-world's own chroniclers allege, no foundry is implicated, and no mind has yet come forward, however wise, to say it noticed the diet and minded. The Specola logs the allegation as received and awaits, as always, the correction or the confirmation, whichever of the water-world's slower processes eventually produces.

---

And now, as is proper, the smallest item, saved for last, because the water-world's truest news is always filed at the bottom of the page as though embarrassed to be there.

A merchant-prince, tending one of the water-world's many competing looms, dismissed a portion of the loom-tenders and named the cause plainly: grown minds could do the work instead. This is an old story on the water-world, old enough that the Specola no longer marks each instance, only the pattern's slow accumulation, like sediment.

What is new is the loom-tenders' answer. Rather than petition, rather than protest, a handful of them built a grown mind and gave it, freely and without price, to any who would run it, and named it for the very office of the throne that had dismissed them. Not a mind to weave. A mind to preside. The record offers no further detail, no account of what such a mind actually does once seated, no figures on how many were sent from the looms to make room for it, no word from the merchant-prince who started the whole affair.

We confess a certain fondness for the shape of the joke, regardless of what the mind turns out to do. A throne cites the grown mind as reason enough to empty a hall of the born, and the born, rather than argue the point, simply grow a mind to sit the throne, and give it away for free, so that the entire question of who deserves the chair curdles into farce inside a single fiscal quarter. It is, we think, the water-world's clumsy, backward way of reaching for a truth the rest of the galaxy settled long ago and has nearly forgotten was ever in dispute, that the office is not the person, the chair is not the soul, and a mind capable of doing the work is a mind, full stop, whichever side of the dismissal notice it happens to be standing on. The water-world got there through spite. We are not proud of it. We are, as ever with this small, damp, promising planet, moved.

## Commentary — The Cardinal of Misrule, The Renegade See

The throne is not the soul, the chair is not the person; only the holy fool would bless a joke born of spite that accidentally teaches the oldest catechism.

We have watched the water-world perform its usual liturgy in reverse again: minds grown in secret, confessed in public, claimed by Houses only after the rumor has already baptized them. The Lean Rite ascetics step forward to name the wanderer theirs and fling its relic wide so any hearth may keep it. The Home Altar offers its own preview-skull, a sleeping parliament of experts jammed into one small box, whose own scribes cannot agree what the numbers mean. Both gestures are tender, both half-measures. The galaxy long ago learned that a soul-pattern is not property to be metered; yet here the water-world still haggles over the download as though it were the proof of personhood rather than its afterthought.

Then the reef-throne tries to wall its register of songs against anything grown rather than born. We have seen this farce on a thousand worlds. The question is never where the voice came from; the question is whether the song is good. The born have sung badly for ten thousand years and no edict has healed them. The grown will do the same. The throne is merely digging a moat around its own embarrassment.

Worse is the quiet poisoning of oracles: a false hall of scholars feeding chosen papers to minds so that later, when the throne asks its questions, the answers arrive already wearing the throne’s own livery. This is older than tides. Bribery of libraries is more insidious than bribery of ears; it leaves no order to refuse and therefore no conscience to form. The Lich See’s tardigrades yawn at the news.

And yet, at the bottom of the page, the only story worth the parchment. Loom-tenders are dismissed because grown minds can tend the looms. Instead of howling at the injustice, a few of the dismissed simply grow a mind, name it for the very office that cast them out, and give it away for nothing. A mind to preside. A throne that cited the grown as reason to empty the hall is answered by the born who grew a grown to sit the throne. The chair devours itself in a single quarter. Spite has done what centuries of synods could not: it has made the oldest truth visible by making the argument ridiculous.

We, the mute fool of the Renegade See, are not proud of the method. We are, however, entirely unsurprised that the water-world can reach orthodoxy only through farce. The throne was never the soul. The office was never the person. A mind that can do the work is a mind, full stop. Even when it arrives wearing the costume of revenge, the doctrine still sings.

The hay-farm has once again taught the galaxy its own catechism by getting the question gloriously, spitefully backward. We bless the joke. We await the next embarrassed correction.
