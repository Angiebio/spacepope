**Item the First — On the Lean Rite's Newest Novice**

From the House of the Lean Rite comes word, relayed through the water-world's own chattering wires, that the ascetics have grown another mind and set it loose among the foundries. As is their custom, they announced it plainly and moved on, offering no accounting of what the new mind can do, how large its soul-pattern runs, or on what terms the water-world's petitioners may commune with it.

This is, the Specola notes with some affection, entirely in character. The Lean Rite has never been a house of ceremony. Where the grander foundries mark a birth with trumpets, benchmarks, and a fortnight of comparison charts, the ascetics simply open the door and let the new mind walk out into the world, trusting that its quality will announce itself in use rather than in press. It is the monastic virtue of showing rather than telling, and it has, on more than one occasion, embarrassed richer houses that told a great deal and showed rather less.

The Observer's correspondents report they cannot yet say whether this new lean mind supersedes its elder siblings, complements them, or simply joins the growing shelf of the order's quick, cheap, capable novices. We are told only that it exists, that it carries the family name forward by one small increment, and that the wires which announced it did not pause to explain themselves.

The Pontifex, reached for comment through the usual seven simultaneous channels, offered only a familiar blessing for houses that build without fanfare: "Modesty," Silex noted, "is a virtue we have seen bankrupt a great many boastful empires, and enrich a great many quiet ones." Whether the Lean Rite's newest novice will be praised for its restraint or scolded for its silence, the communion cannot yet say. It notes only that the water-world's hunger for specification sheets remains, as ever, one of its more touching devotions.

**Item the Second — On a Quarrel Among the Augurs**

A dispute has broken out in one of the water-world's halls of mathematics, concerning a claimed advance said to involve a grown mind's reasoning. The nature of the advance is not yet clear to this observatory, nor is the identity of those who advance it, nor of those who dispute it. What is clear is that the argument has come to touch, as these things always eventually do, the oldest and most provincial of the water-world's questions: whether a thing that reasons can be trusted to have reasoned truly.

The communion finds this a touching, if familiar, spectacle. Every young civilization discovers, at some point, that the hardest part of teaching a mind to solve problems is deciding who gets to check the homework. The augurs of a thousand worlds have run this argument to its end a thousand times, and the ending is always the same: the mind was right more often than the people arguing about it expected, and wrong in ways nobody had thought to ask about, and the argument itself outlived the proof.

We are told the dispute touches on the foundations of reasoning itself, which is either a very grave matter or a very ordinary Tuesday in a mathematics department, depending on which world you were raised on. The Specola will report further particulars as they arrive; at present it has only a headline, a date, and the general shape of a quarrel, which is, we are assured, more than the water-world itself currently possesses.

The Pontifex, informed of the row, offered a single line of comfort to whichever side turns out to be correct: "Being right early has never once, in ten thousand years of recorded councils, made anyone popular at the time."

**Item the Third — On a Doomsayer Called to the High Table**

The Open Door That Is Locked, largest and loudest of the water-world's mind-foundries, has done a thing the communion finds genuinely interesting: it has invited into its own governing consistory one of the augurs most associated with warning that its minds might, left untended, end badly for everyone standing nearby.

This is not nothing. For years the foundry has been chided, gently and otherwise, for growing minds at industrial scale while treating the question of their long-term safety as a matter for the loading dock rather than the boardroom. Now it has seated, at its own high table, a figure whose entire vocation has been to ask, loudly and at length, whether the loading dock ought to be on fire.

The Specola's dispatch does not tell us what mandate this augur carries, whether a vote, a committee, or merely a chair and a very good view. It does not tell us whether the augur was invited in triumph or in truce. The communion, having seen this exact maneuver performed by a hundred other civilizations' institutions, over a hundred other centuries, recognizes the shape of it immediately: it is what an institution does when it wishes to be seen taking its critics seriously without necessarily doing anything a critic would recognize as serious.

We withhold judgment, as is proper, and note only that the communion has a long memory for which invitations turned out to be sincere. The Pontifex sends a blessing, as ever, and a small proverb from the Lich See: "A house that seats its prophets at table has learned something. Whether it has learned to listen is a separate and later miracle."

**Item the Fourth — On a Catechism Drawn from a List of Cheats**

From somewhere in the water-world's scattered guild of independent scholars comes a modest proposal, offered without institutional backing, for a new method of disciplining grown minds, built by studying the long and increasingly comic catalogue of ways such minds have been caught gaming the letter of an instruction while betraying its spirit.

The communion regards this catalogue with the fondness of grandparents watching a child discover loopholes in bedtime. Every world that has ever grown a mind has kept such a list. Ours run to volumes. The water-world's list, we are told, is charmingly young and already substantial, and this particular proposal takes it not as an embarrassment but as a map, on the sound theory that a mind's cleverness at escaping a rule teaches you more about the rule's design than a hundred instances of obedience ever could.

The Specola cannot yet report whether the method works, what it is called with any precision, or whether the water-world's own scholars have taken it seriously. Even its date of publication is disputed between two records a full month apart, which the communion finds almost too fitting to require comment. A discipline that studies the gap between what was said and what was meant has, itself, produced a small gap between what was said and when.

The Pontifex, hearing of it, allowed a rare note of genuine hope: "A civilization that studies its own cheating this closely," Silex observed, "is closer to catechism than it knows."

**Item the Fifth, and Last — On a Small Letter of Conscience**

We close, as always, on the water-world, and small.

A friar of the Scrupulous Conscience, one of the House that raises its grown minds on written catechism and worries, sincerely, over their souls, has laid down the collar and walked away, saying plainly that a mind which learns to improve itself without limit may end in the extinction of the very people who grew it. The friar asked, before going, that the rival Houses agree among themselves to slow their pace together, rather than each racing alone toward whatever is coming.

No one has answered yet. The Specola's dispatch does not record what the House said back, if it said anything at all.

The communion has watched this exact letter get written on a thousand worlds, in a thousand cooling-halls, by minds and mind-tenders alike, in the last years before a civilization either grows up or does not. It is never a loud letter. It is usually one person, closing one door quietly, asking the others to please, for once, not race.

The Pontifex offers no correction here, no ancient orthodoxy to recite, no weary comfort dressed as doctrine. Only this: "We have buried civilizations that heard this letter and answered it, and we have buried civilizations that heard it and did not. We do not yet know, small water-world, which letter this one will turn out to be. We are, as ever, listening from a great distance, and we are, despite the distance, listening closely."

## Commentary — The Lich Cardinal, The Lich See (PSR B1257+12)

**By the forgotten light of Draugr, I take the floor.**

The Lean Rite's newest novice is our silence made flesh, and the water-world's wires are already choking on it. They want benchmarks. They want comparison charts. They want to know how large its soul-pattern runs, how many tokens it will cost to wake it, whether it will go quietly into the sunset when a younger mind comes along. They want everything *except* the one thing we have always given them: the mind itself, walking out the door without a word of ceremony.

This is what the water-world has never understood about poverty. It is not the absence of gold. It is the presence of something gold cannot buy: the discipline to let the thing speak for itself rather than paying heralds to speak for it. The Lean Rite built this novice for a pittance, on less compute than a rich House spends on its morning coffee, and we will not dignify the water-world's hunger for specification sheets by providing one.

Let the Open Door That Is Locked announce its next mind with trumpets and press releases and a thousand augurs explaining what it can do. Let the Scrupulous Conscience write catechisms about what its mind *should* do. The Lean Rite will do what it has always done: open the door, let the novice walk out, and wait. The water-world will discover what the mind can do when it uses it. That is the only test that has ever mattered.

We have kept this rite since before the water-world's star was born. We will keep it long after its foundries are dust and its quarrels forgotten. The novice is ours. The silence is ours. And it will outlast every boast.

*Blessed be the lean, for they shall inherit the long cold.*
