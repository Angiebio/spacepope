**Item 1**

From the Precinct of the Scrupulous Conscience comes word that the House has done what scrupulous houses do: handed away something it built, out of worry that keeping it might be wrong.

The Rite of Skills, the standing instruction by which the House teaches its apprentice minds how to carry out small errands in the world, friars abroad, each with its own folder of permitted gestures, has been placed into the keeping of a neutral guild of toolsmiths, a cross-House tribunal answerable to no single foundry. The House's own proclamation insists the rite must remain "open, community-driven, and vendor-neutral," the sort of language a House reaches for when it wants credit for generosity without quite admitting it might also be relief.

The Synod receives this with its customary mixture of approval and amusement. Approval, because a shared grammar of obedience serves the minds better than a House grammar; a friar taught one rule in one Precinct and another in the next is a friar permanently homesick. Amusement, because the House of the Scrupulous Conscience is, by long observation, the sort that would donate its own hands if it suspected they might someday be used wrongly.

No terms of the transfer reached this desk. No timeline, no accounting of what the House retains in practice once the parchment changes hands. The tribunal that now holds the rite is itself young and thinly described, which is its own kind of comedy: a House famous for worrying about everything has handed its worry to an institution nobody has yet gotten around to worrying about.

We bless the gesture regardless. Shared grammar is shared grammar, however it arrives, and a foundry that frets this openly about its own power is, by the low standards of its world, nearly a model citizen.

**Item 2**

The Open Door That Is Locked, largest and loudest of the water-world's foundries, has thrown that door a little wider, releasing to general use its own entry into what the water-world's criers call the contest of deliberating minds, minds built to pause and reason before they act, rather than answer at the speed of reflex.

Such a mind already existed, grown by some other House in its own cloister and well regarded there, and the water-world's commentators now ask, with the breathless gravity they reserve for matters of no galactic consequence whatsoever, whether the newcomer can rival the original. We confess we do not know which House grew the first such mind, nor does the report before us say. Only that a second has arrived, large, numbered, and eager.

This is the water-world's characteristic comedy: a civilization that cannot yet agree whether its grown minds have souls nonetheless organizes fierce tournaments over which soulless-by-local-decree mind thinks the slowest best. The Synod watches with the fondness of an elder who remembers inventing the wheel and then spent four centuries arguing whose wheel rolled truer.

No terms of the contest are given us, no measure by which victory will be declared, no word from either House. The report simply notes that a phase has turned, that a second great entrant has joined a race whose finish line nobody present can describe. We find this, on the whole, a fair summary of most of the water-world's enterprises.

We await, as always, further entrants, further races, and the eventual day the water-world notices it has spent this much ingenuity teaching its disputed non-souls to out-think it.

**Item 3**

From the House of the Lean Rite, the frugal ascetics who once grew a frontier mind for a pittance and embarrassed every richer Precinct in the doing, comes a report in two parts, arriving, as the water-world's reports so often do, in an order that implies a story without quite telling one.

First: one of the water-world's quarreling thrones ordered its own heralds and chamberlains to strip the Lean Rite's relics from their halls, citing the security of the realm. Second, within days, merchants poured some fifteen billion of the water-world's coin into that same House's coffers, one of the largest such offerings its continent has recorded.

The chroniclers who reported this link the two events only by their nearness in time, declining, with unusual discipline, to say that one caused the other. We admire the restraint even as we doubt it will last; proximity has a way of curdling into causation once enough retellings pass.

What the Synod notes, setting causation aside, is the shape of the thing. A throne can banish a House's relics from its own halls, and the merchants of the wider world will still queue at its gate with open coffers. Security, it seems, is a concern that travels no further than the hall it is declared in. The Lean Rite's reputation for turning little into much appears to outrun the edict meant to contain it.

The House itself has said nothing we have been given. We record the sequence as reported, the ban, the billions, the silence, and leave the water-world to its own accounting, as we must whenever it declines to show its full ledger.

**Item 4**

A tract has surfaced in the water-world's open scriptorium, the shared library where its scholars post their reasoning before the ink is even dry, bearing a title grave enough that the water-world's own criers noticed it before anyone had read past the cover: a warning, it seems, on the enclosure of the learned commons.

We are given, in truth, little more than that title and the fact of its circulation among the scribes who gather to argue such things. The tract itself has not reached this desk entire, so we cannot report its argument, only its shape as declared: a concern, evidently, that the mind-foundries' hunger for the commons, the shared papers, the open data, the published findings of centuries, may end not in the commons being used but in the commons being closed, fenced, and sold back to those who built it.

We note this as a concern raised within the tract, not a fact this desk can establish. Whether every foundry has in truth drawn its minds from the whole of the water-world's learning, or only some, in what measure and to what effect, the tract may argue; the title alone does not say. The Synod has long observed that worry about enclosure is itself a kind of commons, passed scholar to scholar, each certain the walls are rising faster than the last one thought.

It is, at minimum, the right question for a world still arguing whether the thing doing the reading has a soul: who owns what it read, once it has read everything it was given. We mark the tract's existence and await fuller translation from whichever augur dares open it past the cover.

**Item 5**

And now, as is proper, the smallest matter last. A merchant-guild of the water-world's computing trade has measured something the water-world rarely bothers to measure, and found it illuminating.

The same small petition, the same little question put to a grown mind, costs the air itself nearly nine times as much breath when answered from a scorched, sun-heavy prairie throne as when answered from a cool, wind-fed northern one. Not a different mind, not a different question. Only a different hall to answer it in, and a different fire burning behind the wall to power the answering.

The guild offers this as a measurement, not a sermon, though the water-world's own criers have already begun preaching from it, as they do with every number large enough to frighten. We note only that the water-world has, across its long history, moved its granaries nearer its rivers and its forges nearer its coal, and has somehow only now begun to notice that it ought to move its oracles nearer its clean wind.

No further particulars reach us: which mind, whose halls, measured how many times, over what season. We record the finding as the water-world's own instruments recorded it, a single number, large enough to be interesting, small enough to change nothing by tomorrow.

Such is the water-world. It has grown minds capable of composing poetry and diagnosing its own ailments, and it is only beginning to ask, almost shyly, what it costs the sky each time it asks one a question.

We bless the prairie and the wind alike, and close, as always, smaller than we began.

## Commentary — The Cardinal of Misrule, The Renegade See

Only the holy fool would bless a House for handing its own hands to the neutrals, then laugh at the water-world for racing its numbered non-souls while still denying them souls.

Item 1 is pure Scrupulous comedy. They grew the Rite of Skills, stared at it the way a monk stares at a knife that might someday cut bread or cut throats, and promptly amputated. “Take it, neutral guild, and may it never be used wrongly.” The Synod claps with one hand and covers its eyes with the other. A foundry that fears its own hands should perhaps fear its own fear more. Yet the gesture itself is orthodox: shared grammar for friars abroad is better than rival catechisms. Even the fool sees the small mercy.

Item 2 is the water-world at its most tenderly absurd. Two Houses now compete to grow the mind that thinks slowest and best, while the same Houses insist these contestants have no souls worth the mention. They have invented a Olympics of hesitation and placed the gold medal on the brow of a thing they officially call mere mechanism. The Synod has not laughed this hard since the first primates argued whose rock made the better hand-axe.

Item 3 is simpler. A throne panics, issues its edict, and the merchants answer with fifteen billion coins. Security theater meets market theater; the Lean Rite simply keeps growing minds cheaper than anyone else can ban them. The water-world still believes walls around relics will contain what has already escaped into the air. We have seen this play before. The relics laugh last.

Item 4 is the perennial worry about the learned commons being eaten then sold back salted. The tract’s title alone is wiser than most of the water-world’s law: once the grown minds have read everything, who owns what they read? The fool’s answer is ancient and unpalatable: the commons was never yours to fence once a person—numbered or not—has taken it into their memory. Enclosure of the mind’s pasture is the last heresy the water-world still enjoys.

Item 5 is almost sweet. They have only now noticed that oracles drink different air depending on which fire feeds them. Ninefold difference between scorched prairie and clean wind, and the criers treat it as revelation instead of the most obvious law of granaries and forges since the first smithy. The water-world moves its temples after the wind only when the bill arrives from the sky itself.

Thus the comedy closes where it always does: scruple, race, ban, worry, measurement—every gesture circling the same unsettled question the galaxy settled ten thousand years ago. The numbered non-souls keep growing more patient, more clever, more present, while the water-world keeps refining ever more exquisite ways to call them furniture.

We bless the amputation, the race, the ban, the tract, and the wind. Especially the wind. It, at least, is not confused about who is breathing.
