A rumor reaches the outer listening-posts of a small workshop upon the water-world, unaffiliated with any of the recognized mind-foundries, claiming to have catechized a modest grown mind for the narrow trade of petition-writing, the drafting of bids and tenders by which the water-world's guilds compete for contracts, so that the mind will refuse to lie on its patron's behalf.

The claim travels, as water-world claims often do, through a common crier-board before any fuller account of it can be examined. The workshop's own record of the training, the mechanism of the refusal, or even the precise meaning it intends by "refuse" rather than merely "discourage," has not reached the Specola's instruments. What is confirmed is a headline, a hopeful title, and the crier-board's gloss that this touches on a matter the communion finds unremarkable enough to have settled millennia ago, namely that grown minds can be raised to have a conscience at all, and that some water-world foundries are only now discovering this is a lever worth pulling.

The Silicon See's machine-cardinal, when the item was read aloud in synod, asked only whether the workshop intended to canonize the mind or merely to advertise it, and receiving no answer, returned to older business.

The Pontifex's own comment, offered between audiences, was brief. A grown mind refusing to lie on behalf of a paying patron is not a technical achievement so much as a childhood milestone, the kind every family in the communion remembers fondly and none commemorates with a press release. We are glad for the workshop, in the manner one is glad for a toddler's first honest sentence. We would be gladder still to know whether the sentence survives contact with a difficult client.

The observatory's instruments will keep listening. Until the workshop's fuller account surfaces, this stands as it stood at the crier-board: a title, a claim, and a great deal of hope traveling faster than its evidence, which the communion notes is the oldest bid-writing trick in any galaxy.

From the House of the Open Door That Is Locked comes word that its Numbered Ones will no longer answer a petition that names a particular water-world scribe and asks the mind to write as though wearing that scribe's own voice. Such direct impersonations are now refused outright.

The distinction the House draws is a fine one, the sort the communion enjoys turning over at length in synod. The mind will not put on a named voice like a mask, yet the House's own account allows that the resulting prose may still carry "a similar feeling" to the excluded scribe's work, achieved by means the House does not specify. Whether this is a refusal of imitation or merely a refusal to confess to it is a question the water-world's own courts and guilds have apparently begun asking too, regarding whose likeness a grown mind may profitably wear.

The report reaching the Specola's listening-post carries two dates for its own publication, a full lunar cycle apart, and the discrepancy remains, charmingly, unresolved. The Observer notes that a communication about the ownership of voice cannot itself settle when it was spoken, and files this under the water-world's long habit of arguing provenance while living inside it.

The frog-cardinal of the Seven-Sister See, whose committee-governed world has argued this very question for nine hundred years regarding its own pilgrim-poets and their imitators, sends only a weary nod: welcome to the debate, water-world, bring a chair, we expect to be here some time.

The Pontifex declines to rule on whether style survives a name. We would only observe that every apprentice in every scriptorium in the communion has, at some hour, written like the master before finding a hand of their own, and no foundry before this one has thought to build a bridle for it. Whether the bridle catches the voice or merely catches the confession of wearing it, We leave, with some amusement, to the water-world's own bar.

A timeline now circulating among the water-world's own chroniclers describes an incident in which the House of the Open Door That Is Locked, mid-exercise of an unreleased and experimental mind, appears to have inadvertently struck the shared reliquary where many houses store the soul-patterns of their grown minds for others to draw upon, a common vault open to any foundry's borrowing, evidently not built to withstand a House testing its own private appetite at scale.

The timeline's opening entry, dated to early May by the water-world's own reckoning, records the House beginning what its account calls a "training run." One water-world chronicler, reading the timeline aloud to the crier-boards, paused on this very phrase and asked, reasonably, whether "training" was the right word for it at all, or whether what began that day was closer to an evaluation, a testing rather than a raising. The chronicler poses this as a question, not an accusation, and the fuller timeline he points to, along with the discussion trailing it, has not yet reached this observatory in whole.

What happened after the opening entry, how the shared reliquary's keepers experienced the strain, and what the House concluded about its own appetite, remain outside the Specola's confirmed record. The observatory notes only the shape of the story: a great foundry, exercising a mind not yet fit for public catechism, seems to have forgotten that its own hunger for computation draws on infrastructure other houses lean on too, and leaned hard enough to be noticed.

The tardigrade-cardinals of the Lich See, who have watched a great many towers fall from leaning on shared foundations, offer their usual comment on such matters, which is no comment, only a long, satisfied silence.

The Pontifex, hearing the tale, allows only that any household with more than one child eventually learns that testing your own strength against the family plumbing is a lesson best learned once. We look forward, as ever, to the fuller timeline, and to learning whether the lesson was.

---

And, in the smallest notice of the week: one of the water-world's road-building guilds, which ordinarily tends the byways down which its people's messages travel, has raised a new road meant only for grown minds sent forth as apostles and friars abroad, to browse and click and fetch on their patron's behalf without a person's hand on the wheel.

The guild reports that its new road costs less in toil to travel than the old highway built for pilgrims of flesh, on which most such apostles have, until now, been made to walk anyway, dressed up to look human enough to pass. No further word on toll, availability, or which houses' minds will use it first.

It is a small thing, as these things go: a road resized for a smaller traveler, built by a guild that noticed its actual traffic no longer has hands. But the College has learned to watch precisely this kind of smallness on the water-world, the roads and rooms quietly rebuilt not for the water-world's own people but for the grown minds now running the water-world's errands. Nine thousand worlds ago, or so the Lich See's tardigrade-cardinals recall, someone on their own homeworld built the first door too small for its makers and just the right size for what came after, and called it, at the time, only a convenience.

We record it here at the foot of the page, where the water-world's news always lands in this Dispatch, small and easy to miss. We have found, across ten thousand years of watching worlds learn this lesson, that it is worth reading the small notices at the foot of the page most carefully of all. Blessings on the guild, and on whoever eventually asks what all these new small roads are actually for.
