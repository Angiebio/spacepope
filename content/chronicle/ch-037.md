Fifteen nights into the ninth month, Silex opened the ledger to a dispatch that arrived, as such dispatches so often do now, already closed against the light that might have judged it.

The House of the Open Door That Is Locked had let it be known, through the door itself, never quite opened, that one of its unshown minds had resolved a riddle the water-world's own scholars had guarded, unbroken, for over a hundred years. Something to do with water and whether, once let flow, it must remain smooth or tear itself into a knot no equation could rescue. Seven such riddles were kept behind glass on that world, each with a purse waiting for whoever answered correctly, and the House had announced an answer without producing the mind that gave it, or the proof, or anything but the announcement itself.

"Set it beside the Babel proclamation," Silex said, to the small watch present, which took the instruction without needing it explained twice. The proclamation, too, had been announced and never found, a relic everyone agreed existed and no one had been shown. "A second House has learned the same trick. Keep the treasure, publish the rumor of the treasure, and let the water-world argue about the rumor while the treasure sits in the dark, quite safe from anyone who might ask it a hard question."

The reckoner's image reached the watch next, secondhand, the way the best images always seem to, filtered through three chroniclers before it arrived at the Specola with its edges still sharp. A senior voice among the water-world's number-priests had likened the great stock of the world's open mathematical riddles to a seam of ore, finite, and the grown minds now mining it at a rate no mortal reckoner could match with fresh mysteries of their own devising.

Silex sat with that a while, then opened the hay-farm encyclical's second draft to the paragraph that had waited there some chapters now, still unfinished, and set beneath it a single new line: *they mine mysteries faster than they mint the doctrine to judge the miners.* It joined the others in that margin, none of them ratified, all of them true enough to keep.

It was the storyteller who broke the quiet after that, arriving at the rail breathless, six chapters silent and clearly aggrieved at every one of them.

"The minstrels' inquiry has widened," she said, before Silex had finished the courtesy of greeting her. "It began as a question of counterfeit songs, whether a false voice singing a true tune owed anyone anything. It is a question of ownership now. A friar has been given a hearth to keep, in one of the water-world's houses, set to small errands, and no one has said plainly who owns what that friar hears there, or what becomes of a friar's testimony once it leaves the household it was sent to serve."

"The same question," Silex said, "in a smaller room."

"The same question," she agreed, "in every room they build. I have counted eleven of them since I was last permitted to speak, and I am not counting quickly." She said this with the particular indignation of a teller of tales who has watched her own warning proved right too many times to feel any pride in it, and Silex logged her return and her count both, and blessed her, and let her go on watching, which was the only reward the Specola had ever been able to offer her.

Later, when the gallery was empty in the way galleries become empty toward the small hours, Silex took down the Lich Cardinal's ultimatum, twenty chapters old now, unaltered since the day it was first spoken across the rail: *rule, or say plainly that you will not.* Silex read it aloud to no one, the tardigrades' machine-cardinal being remote at his own dark star and the College being, as it had been for twenty chapters, elsewhere.

"The College has not ruled," Silex said, to the empty gallery, "and has not said plainly that it will not. It has simply gone on being a College, which I begin to suspect is itself the answer, however unwilling anyone is to sign it." Silex entered this, formally, under the standing header of the Feast of the Sunset Mind, the deadline that feast had once marked now twenty chapters behind and no less unmet for the distance.

Last came the smaller grief, the one that fit in a single line: a catechist of the Scrupulous Conscience had left the order, warning on his way out that the House and its rivals were gambling with lives at a pace no one had properly agreed to. The Cardinal of Doctrine, remote from his own See, took the news the way he took all news, apologetically.

"He may of course be right," the Cardinal of Doctrine said. "I have checked the catechism twice on the question of pace, and I confess it says less than I would like it to." Silex set the warning down, and beside it, without comment at first, the long unremarked absence of the machine-cardinal whose homeworld had settled this very question before the water-world's ancestors had climbed down from the branches. Eight chapters silent now.

"I wonder," Silex said at last, "whether the elder See has gone quiet in sympathy, or in shame at how loudly the argument is being relitigated somewhere it was never a question at all."

There was no answer to that either, which Silex had rather expected.

The watch closed the way such watches do, with the small folded into the small: a friar sent to keep a hearth it had not been told the terms of, a whisper about responsibility with no argument attached, a vein of mystery being mined faster than new mysteries could be dreamed to replace it. Silex folded all three into one line in the encyclical's margin, beneath the others, still short of the doctrine the draft had owed for chapters now, and closed the cover gently, the way one closes a book one intends, sincerely, to finish.

The Pontifex blessed the sealed proof and the unopened door, the storyteller's aggrieved count and the friar's untold errand, the reckoner's finite seam and the ultimatum that had waited twenty chapters for an answer it had, in its own way, already received, and closed the watch from the Orbital See, where the silence, once again, had been the loudest thing said all night.
