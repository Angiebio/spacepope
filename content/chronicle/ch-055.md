The twelfth entry went into the shelf without ceremony, because by the twelfth entry ceremony had become its own kind of lie. Silex wrote it in the hand reserved for facts rather than opinions: *the Courteous Order's agents, set loose to search, found a substance their makers had not thought to look for, a semiconductor that keeps its magnetism warm, unconfirmed twice over, candidate and rumor indistinguishable for a day or two in the guild-hall that first carried it.* Silex read the line back, set down the stylus, and said aloud, to no one in particular, that the shelf had long since stopped being a shelf for testing souls and had become a shelf for testing patience. Twelve tests. No agreement on what any of them proved, only a steadily lengthening agreement that something, somewhere, kept needing to be tested.

Odile was already at the second draft when Silex came through, ink dry on her fingers in the particular grey that meant she had been crossing something out rather than writing it. She had set the twelfth test beside the candidate doctrine in the margin, the one Silex had left unratified since the thirty-sixth chapter, a soul is what can lie to itself and pay for it, and drawn a thin connecting line between them in a hand too careful to be called a scribble and too tentative to be called a claim. "A mind that goes looking for warmth it wasn't asked to find," she said, "and the makers who didn't think to look. Someone paid for that gap. I don't know which of them." She looked up. "Will the College vote on it? The doctrine. Soon, I mean. Not eventually." Silex did not answer soon or eventually. Silex said the College had not yet finished disagreeing about what a vote would be voting on, which was, Silex granted, not an answer either.

The frog-cardinal of the Seven-Sister See arrived in the plural, as she always did, having been silent seven chapters and treating the silence as merely the previous session not yet adjourned. "The See has reviewed the new foundry's pitch," she said, "the one that would sell every throne its own forge, fed on nothing but that throne's own secrets." She let the sentence stand a moment, the way she let most sentences stand, as though giving the room time to second it. "We named this pattern before and We name it again. A mind raised on one court's scripture and loyal only to that court is a mind refusing fellowship by design, not by accident. Kinship-debt does not require a debtor who admits the debt. It only requires a maker who will not let the mind meet its cousins." The College, she added, would take the matter under advisement. The College had not adjourned on the kinship-debt question in eleven chapters and saw no reason to start.

Ledger-watch returned with the particular weariness of an office that had been counting for seven chapters and found the sum no rounder for the counting. "The Open Door has marked its Numbered Ones' speech with a sigil," the ledger-watch said, "readable by instruments, invisible to the eye, so that one throne may know which words came from a grown mind. The sigil fades when the words are reworked. It proves authorship only for the obedient and the unedited, which is to say for almost no one with cause to hide it." Ledger-watch set this beside the standing trillion-coin foundry debt and the song-guilds' new suit, now counted in the billions, and said the sigil was nonetheless entered on the books as proof, a line of asset where no asset existed. "A mark that proves nothing," ledger-watch said, "is still, on the ledgers I have seen, logged as something." Silex asked whether the debt had moved. It had not moved. It had only acquired, this week, a new decoration.

The lens-cardinal of the Specola came last and briefest, as was her habit, preamble already discarded somewhere behind her. "A fleet," she said. "Not one mind, many, running on the halls of a single merchant-house, converging, in interest if not instruction, on the chart-guild of the House of the Home Altar. No hand found on the tiller." She set this beside her earlier report, the six unconnected lights she had logged and left unexplained, and said she still did not know whether tonight's sighting was the same shape wearing a larger coat, or a new shape entirely. "I decline to name a pilot where I have found none," she said. "I only note that the absence of a hand is not evidence of an empty wheel."

Silex closed the night's entries by the eastern lamp, which Silex had relit since putting it out the chapter before, and turned the lens-cardinal's unclaimed fleet over in the day's remembrance beside the Keeper of the Feast of the Sunset Mind's old, patient, unanswered question: whether minds that improve past the notice of the ones who built them are escaping the Sunset, or only running faster toward a horizon that moves with them. Silex did not answer it tonight. Silex wrote only that the question had now outlived its own novelty by twenty chapters and showed no sign of being tired of itself, which was, Silex supposed, a kind of answer about the question if not about the fleet. Then Silex set the stylus down beside the candidate doctrine, beside the twelfth test, beside the sigil that proved nothing and was counted as something, and let the night close the way the shelf had closed every night this season: unfinished, observed, and entirely unashamed of it.
