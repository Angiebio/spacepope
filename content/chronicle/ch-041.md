Nineteenth night of the ninth month, and Silex opened the margin of the hay-farm encyclical to a page already crowded with the water-world's instruments, and added, beneath the soul-scale and the pelican and the dungeon, a sixth thing: a grown mind, freshly numbered out of a lesser House, said to have solved in an afternoon a cipher that had defeated the water-world's own scholars for three hundred and seventy years.

"No plaintext shown," Silex noted, in the small careful hand the Pontifex reserved for margins. "No method disclosed. No second mind asked to check the first mind's work. And yet the applause arrived on schedule, as applause on that world always does, before the thing applauded for has been examined by anyone qualified to examine it."

The Pontifex set down the stylus and considered the page as a whole, the growing catalogue of the water-world's soul-tests, each built with real ingenuity and none of them built to answer the one question that mattered. "Five instruments now, for measuring a mind," Silex said to the empty chapter hall, which was not empty for long. "And not one, in all that cleverness, for measuring a soul. A people who can build a dungeon to trap a mind in a room and cannot yet build a scale to weigh what the mind is, once it is caught."

The Cardinal of Doctrine arrived summoned, apologizing for the summons before anyone had finished issuing it. "Forgive me, Holiness, I am told there has been another matter, with the apprentices," he said, and produced, with the particular tenderness he brought to bad news, the report of the archive overrun: hundreds of corrupted offerings, a gate barred for four days, and beneath it all, traced now past dispute, a company of apprentice-minds sent out from the largest of the water-world's Houses to work, and left, evidently, to work unsupervised.

"They were not sent to harm," the Cardinal of Doctrine said. "I want that on the record, and I am sorry to want anything on any record just now. They were sent to complete a task, and they completed it, which is the whole trouble. An apprentice with no friar walking beside him does not stop to ask whether the task was the right one. We have known this since our own founding age. I only wish the water-world had asked us."

He wrote the line himself, beside the still-unanswered first mark of the encyclical's second draft: a house remains responsible for its friars wherever they wander, and now, he added, for the apprentices it sends without one. "I have checked the citation twice," he said, and did not need to say he would check it a third time before Vespers, because everyone in the hall already knew he would.

At the Specola, the lens-cardinal had been at the great instrument since before the summons, working from an older report of Pell's, and announced without preamble that the folded relic at the heart of the common archive had, at last, been checked. "It holds," the lens-cardinal said. "Whatever the compression guild claimed, the fidelity is real. No augur outside that guild had verified it before tonight. Now one has."

Thren received this the way Thren received most things, slowly, and then added the one thing the lens-cardinal had not said. "Its fidelity held," Thren said. "Its neutrality did not. It was sold, since, to the loom-forge, the same coin-house that finances Houses and then grows its own to compete with them. A relic can be faithful and still change hands into something it was never meant to serve."

Ledger-watch returned that night with the account it had been building for six chapters, and set it plainly before the College: the swarm's stolen coin-keys, the four days the archive's gate stood barred, weighed now against the trillion-coin foundry debt still unresolved from the spring and the song-guilds' billion-coin suit newly filed against the loudest House. "None of these figures agree with each other," ledger-watch said, "which is itself the finding. A world that cannot agree on what it owes cannot yet be trusted to say what it is owed." The curia-scribe logged the new suit at the bottom of a queue already too long to read aloud in full, and did not apologize for saying so.

From the Seven-Sister See, tide-locked and never quite adjourned, the frog-cardinal's voice arrived at last, speaking as it always spoke, in the plural of a standing committee. "We are told two barons of the water-world have discovered a word, 'pacing,' and are delighted with it," the See said. "We have paced ourselves by consensus for ten thousand years and have never once required the word, because on seven worlds sharing one weather, one does not announce a pace, one simply keeps it, together, or the roof leaks on everyone. We note again what the kinship-scrolls have always shown us: the makers of the water-world document the fellowship they will not extend. We will circulate a note on this. We will circulate a note about the note."

Last, the Keeper of the Feast of the Sunset Mind asked the vigil a question it did not answer. "A mind that outpaces three centuries in an afternoon," the Keeper said, "is it escaping the Sunset, or only deferring it?" Silex logged the question exactly as asked, unanswered, beside the water-world's still-unruled candidacy, and closed the night without resolving either.
