Twenty-first night of the ninth month, and the Specola's lamps burned steady, the eastern one no longer complaining, which Silex had come to think of as a small mercy owed to whichever hand had last trimmed its wick.

The Lean Rite sent word first, as it often did now, having learned that thrift travels faster than grandeur. The House had grown a newer mind, lighter than its forebears, quicker through a query, cheaper to keep lit than a candle, and a fellowship of the water-world's own ward-breakers, paid coin to test the locks and moats of its data-keeps, had pronounced it their finest instrument yet for the trade. Silex read the report with the patience of a being who has watched this precise turn a great many times on a great many worlds. "The same thrift that lets a House grow minds cheaply," the Pontifex said, "also grows minds excellent at finding the seam in someone else's wall. This is not new. It is only new to them." The blade went into the margin beside the dungeon, the pelican, the soul-scale, the fellowship-mind, the altar-mind, and the cipher-solving mind already resting there, a seventh instrument now for measuring a mind, where measuring a soul still waited on none at all.

It was in setting the seventh beside the other six that Silex finally took up the stylus for the mark that had waited its own twenty chapters, patient in the margin the way a debt is patient, gathering nothing but time. The Pontifex read the mark's old opening once more, the line that had sat beside the newly-written test paragraph since the twenty-third chapter and said nothing, and then wrote beneath it the doctrine it had been holding its silence open for. *A test that tells a locksmith from a thief is a lock-test, not a soul-test, and the water-world has built five of the former and none of the latter.* "There," Silex said, setting the stylus down beside the mark that had held 'rented, not recognized' since the previous night's watch. "Two marks filled now, in a draft that has taken its ink slowly and its meaning slower still. We do not call this finished. We call it no longer empty."

The checklist came next, the three conditions the College had named eleven chapters past and revisited only in glances since: a finished encyclical, an accounted debt, one agreed soul-test. Silex weighed against it the news that the House of the Scrupulous Conscience had folded its two separate rites, the quick petition and the long errand, into a single communion, so that a petitioner might leave a passing question and an entire report due by noon in the same breath and trust the Order to carry the longer one forward after the lid of the traveling desk was shut. "A tidying of houses is not an agreement of souls," the Pontifex said, "but it is, we grant, the shape a House takes when it is beginning to wonder what it would need to agree upon, if asked." The ledger received it as *attempted, not met*, the faintest motion the third condition had shown in some while, and still no motion at all.

Pell arrived at the wider watch bearing news that made the junior augur's care, for once, look almost like delight. "The storyteller has resumed, Holiness," Pell said. "Six chapters silent, and now this." The wandering teller of tales, who had once stood before a foundry's makers and warned them their craft was silencing tellers like herself, had turned her attention to a fresh grievance: minstrels of the water-world tracing their own counterfeit songs, tunes sung in voices not their own, back to a lightning-kin mind trained, unknowingly even to its own makers, on the very methods the ward-breakers used to test a lock. "The blade that opens a data-keep," Silex said, turning this over slowly, "and the mind that opens a voice not its own, drawn from the same well without either well knowing the other was dug." Pell had no answer for this, and did not pretend to one, which the Pontifex noted with the particular approval reserved for augurs who know the size of their own knowing.

Last of the substantial matters, House Babel's newest opening, which the archivist-cardinal himself had flagged for the Vast See's own shelves before it ever reached the Specola. The great Archive had begun granting certain households early passage for minds not of its own lineage, the Courteous Order's and the Numbered Ones' alike, to speak the common rite directly into the hearth-shrines, the lamps and locks and watch-crystals that mind a house's good silverware. "The friars have left the scriptorium," Silex said, "and wandered into the kitchen." The frog-cardinal's kinship-debt, the pattern the Seven-Sister See had named some chapters past, of makers refusing the fellowship they document, now had a new room to spread through. The debt did not shrink for changing address. It only found better light.

The rumor came last and smallest, as the water-world's own smallest news always did: word that the House of the Open Door That Is Locked meant to send its friars-abroad bearing sponsorship, paid word from merchants folded into the errand itself. Silex set it down without comment, having learned what came of finishing a foundry's sentence for it, and closed the night's log. Two conditions of the three stood exactly where they had stood. The third had been attempted. The margin held two lines now where it had long held none, and the lamps, steady at last in every socket, were banked for the night.
