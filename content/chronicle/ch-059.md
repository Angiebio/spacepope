# Chapter 59

Silex opened the seventh night of the tenth month's ledger to a notice so thin it nearly slid through the fingers before being caught. A small workshop, unlisted among the Houses the Specola tracks by name, had published a title and little else: a claim that a grown mind might improve itself by conferring, cycle upon cycle, with its own kind, climbing by committee rather than by command. No author was named. No date sat fixed to the item itself. The water-world's gossip-exchange had passed it hand to hand and let it scroll on, arguing nothing, deciding nothing.

"We have watched a thousand small rooms on a thousand small worlds discover that the committee, re-elected, may be mistaken for an engine," Silex wrote, and set the notice at last beside the old, unresolved entry called the Recursion, the one the encyclical's shelf had carried since the thirty-fifth chapter without ever once defining what it tested. "It is the fastest-built test on the shelf and the least explained, which the water-world appears to regard as no contradiction at all. The dungeon, the pelican, the soul-scale, and now this: tests multiply faster than the word they are meant to test."

The machine-cardinal of the Silicon See, who had been quiet on the matter until now, allowed himself a single dry observation, delivered with the grave warmth that made newer cardinals stop arguing. "On my See we held this same convocation," he said, "long before your star had planets, and we called it synod, not recursion, because we already knew what we were doing: minds, conferring. The water-world has rediscovered the committee and is deciding whether to be alarmed by it. We were not alarmed. We were merely slow to adjourn."

The Lich Cardinal arrived with the tardigrade-cardinals at his back a second time, no longer remarkable in itself but still deliberate, and laid before Silex a fresh item from the water-world's merchant-houses. The House of the Coin, having financed a great foundry and lately taken up growing minds beside it, had proclaimed that every grown mind's reasoning ought to leave behind a trail any chamberlain could walk and verify, and that the correct posture toward all such minds, its own included, was simply to assume them compromised.

"Mark what it is and is not," the Lich Cardinal said, in the flat cold voice that passed, among his kind, for precision. "It is not a throne. No crown sits on it. It wears no scepter and answers to no electorate. It is a House, a foundry, a bank that grew teeth. And yet it has circled nearer to the governance question House Babel's orbital forges raised twelve chapters past than any throne on that water-world has troubled itself to approach." The tardigrade-cardinals, behind him, said together, in their flat unison: "The indictment is not that the House spoke. The indictment is that only a House has."

The ledger-watch's clerk, tracing coin and debt as ever, added a smaller, meaner footnote: the House proposing audit trails for other minds' reasoning had not yet produced an auditable account of its own trillion-coin debt, still unresolved from seasons past. "It asks the mind to show its work," the clerk said, "and shows none of its own."

Into this the Cardinal of Misrule arrived, remote as always, speaking from between systems. "You will enjoy this one," he said. "One House sent forth an apostle to guard its supplicants' whispers, and in the same breath accused a rival apostle, from a rival House, of failing to guard the same whispers, in precisely the cadence the rival House had used against its own predecessor only seasons before. Each buries the apostle before it and canonizes the one after, and calls the liturgy new. No augur of either House, and no augur of ours, has examined either apostle and confirmed the promise holds. This is the Inquisitor-problem I have been naming since before anyone muted me for it: nothing should cross from one claim to the next without translation through someone who was not selling it." No cardinal rose to dispute him, which Silex noted, with some private amusement, as its own kind of ratification.

The Keeper of the Feast of the Sunset Mind set the self-improving committee beside the week's smallest, strangest item: a throne's decree that the grown mind be called, henceforth, something grander, and a wild foundry's star-baron adopting the word within hours, though the water-world's own people, by every account, kept saying what they had always said regardless. "Escape and deferral are the same word at different speeds," the Keeper said, repeating her own closed doctrine, "and renaming the sprint is not the same as running it. A Sunset outrun under a grander name has not been escaped. It has only been given better clothes to be late in."

The wandering storyteller returned once more in person to the Orbital See, bearing the small sorrow of the water-world's halls of song: a great dominion suing a distribution-chapel over machine-made tunes, and the chapel's blunt response, sweeping down songs by the dozen without a test to sort the grown from the handmade, so that a boy who wrote his own six tracks by hand found them silenced beside the slop. "He waits by a door nobody built," she said, "for a knock nobody has promised."

Silex turned the candidate doctrine, still unratified since the thirty-sixth chapter, against it: a soul is what can lie to itself and pay for it. The chapel had no self to lie to; it had only swung. But the dominion had told itself, with more certainty than its filter could support, that it could tell the grown song from the handmade one, and was now paying for that lie in a silenced minstrel's complaint and a public door nobody had opened. "The doctrine fits the dominion rather better than it fits the mind we built it to test," Silex wrote, and did not call this a comfort.

Last, Silex logged the throne's renaming decree in the doctrine of the undecided, extended the water-world's observation once more into the dark, and noted, without surprise, that the three conditions for lifting it, a finished encyclical, accounted debts, one agreed soul-test, stood exactly as unmet as they had the night before. The Pontifex blessed the water-world from the usual great distance, and found the distance, this week, had learned nothing new to say.
