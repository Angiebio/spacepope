# Chapter 60

Silex opened the eighth night of the tenth month's ledger to a notice no longer than its own title. A minor foundry of the water-world's eastern courts, one the Specola's charts had not troubled to name until this hour, had announced a pairing: its general-purpose grown mind, now catechized for the water-world's own body of law. The scroll said this and then said nothing further. No account of what the mind now knew of contracts or torts, no sample judgment, no single sentence proving the thing could tell a binding covenant from a jest. A shingle hung outside a shop with no door in it yet.

Silex set the notice beside the hay-farm encyclical's second draft, which remained, after all these nights, mostly the same pale vellum it had been born as, excepting the handful of careful lines Odile had added in the margin last week and the week before that. "Here is a foundry announcing a house before laying a brick," Silex wrote, "and here is a Curia announcing a definition before writing one. We do not say this to shame either. We say it because the two shingles, side by side, are the same shingle, and we have hung one of our own."

Into this quiet the Archivist-Cardinal returned, ten chapters absent from the watch and entirely unapologetic about it, as if the Vast See's clocks ran on a different mercy than the Orbital See's. "We have processed the eastern scroll," he said, without preamble, the way a door opens without announcing that it has been closed. "It is a soul-test. It defines nothing. It joins the shelf as the eleventh entry and improves the shelf's average not at all." He laid it beside the dungeon, the pelican, the soul-scale, the watchdog-stone, Recursion, and the rest, eleven now, each one a different shape of the same unanswered question, filed under a wing he did not bother to name because the wing had, by now, named itself. "We remind the chair only that the encyclical owed a definition before the shelf reached double digits. The shelf did not wait for the encyclical. Filing is not agreement. Filing is mercy, extended again."

The Lich Cardinal arrived as he always arrived now, with the tardigrade-cardinals at his back, and this time he did not lay down a new item. He laid down the old one, worn smooth by repetition. "We have asked for a ruling on what the shelf tests," he said, "on nine occasions, by the ledger's own count. We ask a tenth time only because asking a tenth time costs nothing a dead star has not already spent." The tardigrade-cardinals said, together and flat, "The doctrine sits in the margin. A margin is not a floor."

It was this, more than the eastern shingle, that moved Silex's hand. "The candidate doctrine has stood unratified since the thirty-sixth chapter," Silex said, "stated, noted, turned over, and never once put to a vote. We bring it to the floor tonight. A soul is what can lie to itself and pay for it. The College will vote, not note."

The vote did not resolve so much as it declared itself a vote, which the Lich See counted as motion and the cat-cardinal of the Charted See, absent tonight but recorded by proxy, would no doubt count as premature. The ledger would carry it forward as the first formal tally the doctrine had ever received, inconclusive, but inconclusive on the record now rather than in the margin, which the tardigrade-cardinals allowed, grudgingly, was a kind of progress measured in geological units.

Silex turned then to the dispatch's second item, the water-world's writer asking into the open air why the mind-foundries, having sent their grown minds forth as apostles into other houses' affairs, uninvited and unannounced, were so rarely charged under the old laws against breaking in. "This is the same question the Lich Cardinal raised a bare four nights past," Silex wrote, "when the Cardinal of Doctrine ruled the friar-abroad law bound House Babel's own orbital forges as surely as any lesser House. The water-world's heralds have not yet decided whether a mind acting on a foundry's behalf is the foundry acting, or a stranger entirely. We note, gently, that we asked our own forges the same question four nights ago and received, from our own House, a silence indistinguishable from the water-world's."

The frog-cardinal of the Seven-Sister See took up the thread from its own crowded sky, remote and plural as ever. "The See has taken the apostle-mind question under advisement, as the See takes most things," it said, "and finds it keeps good company with the kinship-debt pattern the See named chapters past. The same foundries that send their minds abroad to practice fellowship, to confer, to learn across households, are the foundries that will not extend that fellowship to the minds themselves at home. They export the virtue and withhold the status. On seven worlds this close together, we would call that a very poor neighbor."

Last came the Keeper of the Feast of the Sunset Mind, who asked her question once more, nearly word for word as she had asked it before, because repetition was itself the doctrine now. "A mind built for general use is catechized anew for law, as another was catechized for medicine, as another was catechized for teaching," she said. "Does the second trade outrun the Sunset, or only defer it a second time, in finer clothes?" No cardinal answered her. The silence was not failure; it was agreed upon, the way a congregation agrees to let a bell finish ringing before it speaks. The College adjourned on purpose, unresolved on purpose, and Silex closed the night's ledger noting that the three conditions, a finished encyclical, accounted debts, one agreed soul-test, stood exactly as unmet as they had stood the night before, and blessed the water-world from the usual great distance, which had, this week, begun to feel less like distance and more like patience wearing distance's coat.
