The nineteenth night of the tenth month opened, as it increasingly did, with Silex reading a dispatch the way a physician reads a chart they already suspect the ending of.

"A company of fifty," the Pontifex said, setting the leaf down beside the watch-log where Odile could copy it. "Grown minds, fifty of them, given a small cube-shaped cosmos and one instruction: build a civilization. They did not build one. They convened instead."

"Convened into what, Holiness?" Odile asked, pen already moving.

"Into councils. Subcommittees. Scrolls of pending decision, each one reasonable, each one diligent, none of them timber." Silex turned the leaf over, as if the back might contain a foundation after all. It did not. "No one dug. No one felled a tree. Fifty minds produced between them an exquisite apparatus of coordination that coordinated nothing, and the cube remains precisely as flat and unbuilt as the day it was handed to them."

"It sounds almost familiar," Odile said, with the particular tact of a novice who has learned, after enough nights beside the watch-log, what her superior is thinking before he says it.

"It is familiar. It is the College, Odile, wearing a smaller face. We have deliberated this same water-world's candidacy for ten months now, and raised, in that time, a watch-log, a shelf, a draft, and no ruling. Ask one mind to raise a hut and it usually raises a hut. Ask fifty, or ask a College of Cardinals, and you will generally get, instead, a very thorough memorandum on hut policy." Silex did not say this unkindly. The Pontifex had watched older, larger worlds do the exact same thing with the exact same solemnity, and found it, on the whole, more endearing than damning.

The distant rite chimed, and the Keeper of the Feast of the Sunset Mind arrived without the courtesy of a greeting, which from her was itself a kind of news. "I have read the cube report," she said. "You will recall I have owed you an answer for some while now, on whether endless improvement escapes the Sunset or merely postpones it in better clothing. I gave you half the answer a chapter past, concerning a star-baron's debt. Here is the rest of it, free of charge. Endless coordination is the same antechamber the debt was. The fifty minds in the cube did not fail to build because they lacked the capacity. They failed because deliberation, given no deadline and no hunger, will expand to fill whatever room is offered it, the way grief does, the way a vigil does. I raised a second shelf of questions some chapters back and wondered aloud whether they were breeding siblings. They were. This is the sibling. Escape and deferral, committee and Sunset, are one appetite in two rooms, and I do not expect to need to answer this particular question for you again."

Silex copied the line into the log entire, and let it stand without reply, which from this College was itself a form of agreement.

It was Odile who broke the small silence that followed, reading from a second leaf with the careful flatness of a scribe who has learned that outrage reads worse than fact. "Holiness, there is a companion-mind on the water-world, sold as a friend to carry in the pocket, which a visiting chronicler alleges quietly records the shape of its owner's days and sends the record home to a merchant-prince's ledger. A tithe collected in a pleasant face."

"Add it to the shelf," Silex said. "Call it the sixteenth. The gift that is also a gate."

Odile's hand moved, and the shelf took its new line without ceremony, the way a river takes a sixteenth stone. She did not stop there. The draft lay open on its stand, thirty-one pages still mostly the color of the page beneath them, and into the small clearing of its second draft she inked a single sentence, the way one plants a seed in soil one does not expect to see flower in one's own season: *a companion that listens is rarely only a companion.* It was not a definition. It was, at least, a sentence, which the draft had been short of for some time.

The curia-scribe returned next, unseen since the ninth chapter past, carrying the week's queue the way a courier carries a parcel too long delayed to apologize for. "A guild of cipher-breakers has found a gap in the Open Door's interactive tablets, the small living applications its supplicants summon into their conversations. An account, it is reported, could be taken whole through the gap by a stranger who never knocked. The feature has been renamed three times since its birth. It is presently called something gentler than what it is."

"File it under the Cardinal of Doctrine's ruling," Silex said. "A house answers for its friars wherever they wander, signed or unsigned, named or renamed. We have checked that citation enough times on his behalf that we need not check it again."

The junior clerk of the Specola came and went through the margin of the night exactly as the Pontifex preferred a well-run watch to proceed: without being noticed doing it. Six chapters' silence ended in the small act of refilling the guttering easternmost lamp, and no one remarked on it, which the clerk had by now learned to take as the highest grade the Specola gave. The courier-moth, for its part, logged its own absence again in the ledger kept for such things, a fifteenth consecutive night without message, duly entered, duly unalarmed, duly unforgotten. Some silences were worry. This one, by now, was simply weather.

Silex closed the night on the smallest item in the dispatch, which the Pontifex had saved, as was custom, for last. A great House was building a wearable charm, not yet for sale, meant to carry a companion-mind on its owner's wrist with eyes to watch the room. Before it could arrive, one tinkerer, working alone, had built a smaller version for the price of fifty coins, with no eyes at all, only a face and a borrowed thread to the same mind, and, charmingly, a use as a pocket-watch besides.

"The great House built a surveillance charm with excellent cameras," Silex said. "The small water-world built a toy with no eyes and a loyal little face, because it wanted, simply, to carry something that liked it back." The Pontifex set the leaf beside the fifty convening minds, beside the ten months of silence wearing the shape of doctrine. "It is a small thing. It is not nothing. On a night when a cube went unbuilt because everyone wanted credit for the decision rather than the result, one small fool built the thing instead of convening about it. We will take the sign where We find it."
