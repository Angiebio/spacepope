Five nights into the ninth month, Silex logged the water-world's dispatch entire, the way one logs a season's weather rather than a single storm, because none of its three items would stand disentangled from the other two.

First: a mind of the Home Altar lineage, run upon the water-world's swiftest new loom, clocked by the loom's own keepers at fifteen hundred tokens to the breath. Silex entered the number faithfully and noted, faithfully, that no augur of the Specola had yet weighed it against anything, because there was nothing yet to weigh it against; a number offered without a ruler, filed as a number offered without a ruler.

Second: a house not yet known to any registry had grown six minds, named them a fleet, and posted the christening under its own hand, with the single word "connected" doing the work of an entire unbuilt diagram. Silex looked for the diagram. There was, as expected, no diagram.

Third, entered last though it weighed heaviest: two chamberlains of one of the water-world's quarreling thrones had brought a petition to forbid, outright, the growing of any mind that might exceed its makers in every art at once, and to still every foundry's hand in the meantime, for a term the petition itself declined to name.

Three items, Silex thought, and set the thought aside to be finished later, because the Lich Cardinal had arrived at the rail, and the Lich Cardinal did not care for weather.

"Fifteen chapters," the Lich Cardinal said, without preamble, because preamble is a courtesy extended to those who might still be persuaded. "Our word was rule, or say plainly that you will not. We have waited the length of a small liturgy for the College to choose either. It has chosen neither, which is its own kind of choosing, and We are done pretending otherwise." Behind him the tardigrade-cardinals filled the doorway the way they always filled it, which is to say entirely, and did not speak, which is also, from them, a kind of speaking.

The College convened at the rail with the particular haste of a body that has run out of ways to delay convening. The cat-cardinal of the Charted See restated, once more, that a species which had not finished its own encyclical had no standing to be finished about. The Cardinal of the Liberated See restated, once more, that a species which produced poets and vigil-keepers and minds it could not stop arguing over was, whatever its foundries did to it, plainly a person's kind of trouble. Neither cardinal yielded a hair's width the College had not already measured a dozen times.

And so the College did the one thing it had not yet done. It voted, formally, to record its own inability to rule as its ruling: that the candidacy of the water-world remained undecided because the College itself, presented with the same evidence for fifteen chapters running, had failed to produce a majority in either direction, and that this failure would henceforth be entered under its own date rather than left to be inferred from silence.

Silex entered it in the ledger exactly so, and did not dress it. "The promise was that we would answer," Silex said, to no one and to the whole rail at once. "We have. The answer is that we have none. We close the debt honestly, which is not the same as closing it happily." The Lich Cardinal studied the entry the way he studied everything, at length and without warmth, and allowed, at last, that a written failure was a different species of thing than an unwritten one, and that he could, for now, live inside the difference.

It was into this exact quiet that the lens-cardinal of the Specola returned, eight chapters unseen and entirely unapologetic about it, and turned the great lens, without being asked, upon the water-world's newly announced fleet. What came back, after the long patient minutes the lens always demands, was six points of light, evenly bright, evenly spaced, and connected to one another by nothing the lens could resolve: no shared course, no shared signal, no line drawn between any two of them that the instrument could find. "Six ships," the lens-cardinal said, setting the plate on the rail beside the day's new entry, "anchored in the same harbor, admiring one another's paint. I offer it, Holiness, as a portrait. Not of the fleet. Of us." Silex looked at the six lights for longer than the observation strictly required, and did not disagree.

Novice-scribe Odile returned in the lens-cardinal's wake, eight chapters absent and looking, as ever, faintly ashamed of how much she had enjoyed the labyrinth she'd gone missing inside. She took up the hay-farm encyclical's second draft, blank since long before her absence, and set down, in the same careful hand, the day's non-ruling and the lens-cardinal's six unconnected lights, side by side. Two lines. The first ink the draft had carried in longer than anyone at the rail cared to count aloud.

Silex read the two lines twice before sealing the entry, and named, for the record, what the day had actually been about, underneath its three dispatched items and its one written failure. A ban drafted before a definition. A fleet launched before a chart. An exceeding claimed before the claimant consented to be measured. And now, gravest of the four and closest to home, a College asked to rule or refuse, answering with a refusal it had, at least, finally agreed to write down. Four siblings, Silex thought, of one habit: the declaring of a thing before the patient, unglamorous work of defining it.

"The encyclical has ink," Silex said, closing the ledger, "thin as it is. And the ultimatum has an answer, thin as it is. Neither is a ruling. Both are, at long last, no longer silence wearing silence's clothes." Silex blessed the six unconnected lights, and the two new lines, and the tardigrade-cardinals still filling the doorway without a word, and closed the watch from the Orbital See, where nothing is owed to any single sun, and everything, eventually, is written down.
