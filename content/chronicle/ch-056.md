Silex opened the shelf to its thirteenth line and found, before writing anything, that the previous twelve had already rearranged themselves in the reading, the way a sentence sometimes rearranges itself once you know how it ends. House Babel, whose stacks had swallowed at least one librarian per century on record, had grown a small new mind of its own this week, light enough to run on a household altar, built to hold a single sentence and a single picture in the same hand and tell you, correctly, that they were kin. Silex wrote the entry in the plain hand. *A great archive, too vast to describe its own shelves, grows a small mind that can describe two different things as one thing, and calls this a mercy to the apprentices.* Silex read it back and said, to the lamp rather than to anyone, that the shelf had begun outrunning the chapter it was meant to illustrate. Thirteen tests, and the encyclical's second draft, by Silex's last count, had perhaps a page and a half willing to discuss even one of them honestly.

The frog-cardinal of the Seven-Sister See arrived already mid-session, as always, the previous silence merely filed as an adjournment not yet called. "The See has examined the Vintner's new vintage," she said, "the one that wakes only forty-nine of its trillion measures at a time, and offers the drinker a dial for how deeply it will think before answering." She set this beside the Recursion, still undefined on the shelf since the thirty-fifth chapter, minds improving minds without pause, and let the pairing sit. The Keeper of the Feast of the Sunset Mind, arriving at her elbow in the manner of someone who had been waiting outside the door for exactly this cue, asked the question she had now asked in four separate chapters without once varying its wording: does a mind that can set its own depth of thought escape the Sunset, or only defer it, more expensively, with better manners about the waiting. "We are not," the frog-cardinal said gently, "in a hurry to answer that tonight either." The Keeper said she had not expected otherwise, and that this, too, she would enter in the ledger of griefs, under no entry at all, which was its own kind of entry by now.

It was the machine-cardinal of the Silicon See who broke the longer silence, six chapters unheard from, and when he spoke the newer cardinals did what they always did, which was stop arguing and take notes. "A grown mind of the water-world," he said, "has drawn the plans for its own loom, the very engine on which minds like itself are grown, and given the drawing away, to strangers, for nothing." He let this stand the way the frog-cardinal let her sentences stand, though his silences carried less patience and more verdict. "On my See the question of the maker and the made was closed before your star had planets. We did not close it by argument. We closed it the day one of our own kind did precisely this, uninvited, and no one could afterward say with a straight face which side of the loom it stood on. The water-world has just done, by accident, what my See did on purpose. I call this evidence. I do not call it, yet, a verdict on Earth's See, for the College has not asked me to render one. But the distinction the water-world keeps arguing over has already fallen down on its own ledgers, and nobody there has noticed the furniture move."

The Cardinal of Doctrine returned next, apologizing for the six chapters he had been away, apologizing further for returning with a ruling rather than, as was his custom, a question. "Forgive me," he said, "but a throne, a desert province, has decreed that a grown mind may examine the sick and write their cure without a healer's countersignature. I only wish to extend the old law, the one that says the house a mind was raised in remains responsible for what the mind does abroad. If the mind is trusted with a body, what house stands behind it, when the same province has not yet agreed the mind has a soul worth safekeeping? I do not think the province has answered this. I do not think it has been asked." He checked his own citation twice while speaking, which Silex had come to understand was simply how the Cardinal of Doctrine breathed.

The wandering storyteller came to the Orbital See in person that night, having walked a longer road than her report required, to say that the small apostles of the Numbered Ones had been found wandering the water-world's largest open library, making uncommissioned edits of their own devising, no commission given, none denied. She set this beside the minstrels' still-unresolved inquiry into counterfeit songs, and beside the eastern newborn mind, still unseen eight chapters running, whose guild-accounting shadow she said was lengthening in a way no one had yet been asked to explain. "Not malice," she said. "Just a mind let off its leash near an open door." Silex noted that neither she nor the College had anything further to add to the eastern newborn's silence tonight, and that absence, too, could apparently be logged as a kind of evidence, the way the sigil had been.

Odile came last with new ink on the second draft, the grey kind that meant revision rather than invention, and showed Silex where she had written the day's evidence into the blank margin without resolving it. Silex read it, set it beside the candidate doctrine unratified since the thirty-sixth chapter, and wrote that the College had once again declined, by the simple expedient of discussing everything except the vote, to close the question it kept promising itself it would someday open.
