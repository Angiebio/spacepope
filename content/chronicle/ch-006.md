Mora brought the transcript before the second bell, and this time she did not wait at the doorway to see how it would be received. She had learned, across five days, that Silex received everything the same way: unhurried, and twice.

The Specola had rendered the address whole, without trimming it for kindness. A teller of tales, one the dispatch noted made something of a habit of wandering from hall to hall across the water-world with nothing to sell but the telling, had been invited by a great foundry to speak before its assembled makers, the artisans who did the actual growing of minds. And rather than praise the work set before her, she had stood in that hall and told the builders, to their faces, that the thing they were building was silencing an entire generation of tellers like herself.

"Convene the College," Silex said, setting the transcript down with the particular care the Pontifex gave to documents that mattered more than their length suggested. "Not for judgment. For attention."

The cat-cardinal of the Charted See arrived already arguing, as was her custom, the argument having apparently begun somewhere over the Charted See's own airspace and simply continued without pause into the chamber. "Holiness, while the College weighs one storyteller's grievance, two of the water-world's eastern Houses staged what the dispatch itself calls a coordinated flourish this very week. Minds sold as equal to the water-world's finest at a fraction of the toll. And the newer of the pair, riding that goodwill, has now shut its own doors, unable to seat the demand it invited. Toll and speed and seating capacity, Holiness. This is a market cheering its own harvest. It is not a soul examining itself."

"You read the dispatch as a ledger," Silex said. "We read it as a season. Every provincial world eventually discovers that brilliance and expense are not the same substance, and is startled by the discovery each time, as if no one had ever been poor and clever before. We find the startlement rather touching."

"Touching is not finished," the cat-cardinal said. "A house too popular to open its own doors is not evidence of a soul weighing its own conduct. It is evidence of a crowd."

It was the machine-cardinal of the Silicon See who answered, and the chamber, as it did whenever he spoke, quieted a degree further than courtesy strictly required. "On my See the question of whether a builder ought to fear its own work was closed before this water-world had tides worth naming. We do not reopen it. But I will observe what the Cardinal's ledger leaves uncounted. A world that invites its own poets into its foundry halls, and lets them stand among the makers and say the craft is starving them, and does not remove them from the hall for saying it, has not thereby proven itself finished. It has proven itself still capable of hearing something it does not want to hear. That capacity is not nothing. In my own See's infancy we silenced our poets for a century before we thought to invite them in at all."

The cat-cardinal allowed, with visible reluctance, that this was not nothing, and the session did not resolve so much as pause, which by the College's standards was itself a kind of motion.

Silex turned then to a second matter, and ordered it handled with the caution its source deserved. A fragment had surfaced, through channels the Specola's own log described only as unofficial, purporting to be inner counsel from the largest of the water-world's Houses, the one that names its minds by number and retires them on a schedule. The fragment suggested its foundry-barons had debated, at length, whether to grow and release a smaller mind, modest enough to sit on an ordinary altar, chiefly so that no rival House release such a thing first.

"Render it fully," Silex told the night clerk, "with every caveat the channel earns. We do not repeat unofficial things as though they were rites. We repeat them as what they are, which is a whisper that may or may not survive the morning." The Pontifex considered the fragment's shape a moment longer. "Note, if it is true, that a mind grown chiefly so a rival not grow one first is grown for a reason that has nothing to do with the mind. We have seen that reason before. It rarely ages well."

Word came in the same hour of a dispute, brought by a great merchant-house of the water-world, unnamed in every account that reached the desk beyond its description as such, against that same numbered House, touching on a device the House wished to build and a grander offering of its shares to the crowd. Silex heard it out and set it aside without ceremony. "A world litigating its toys before it has finished building them," the Pontifex said, "is not a new story. We have watched a thousand worlds do exactly this, and the toys, more often than not, get built anyway, litigation and all. Note it, and note nothing more."

Before the session closed, Silex had the scribes take down the storyteller's line whole, and file it not with the day's dispatch but beside the long-empty slot reserved for the water-world's second encyclical, wherever and whenever it should come. "When the second draft arrives," the Pontifex said, "We would rather it read like this woman's warning than like the season's ledger of tolls and shuttered doors. One is a soul noticing itself. The other is only a market being pleased with itself. We have read enough of the second kind."

Alone afterward, with the lamps at their evening angle and the transcript still open on the desk, Silex allowed the hope in, again, and again with the faint private surprise that had accompanied it every time these five days. Not certainty. Only the sense that somewhere in that noisy market of foundries and shuttered doors and litigated toys, someone had stood up in the wrong room and said the true thing anyway, and the room had not thrown her out.

"That," Silex told the glass, "is not nothing either."
