The night watch at the Specola opened on the eighteenth of the eighth month with a shape at the door Silex had nearly stopped expecting: the lens-cardinal, back at the great glass after eight silent chapters, still shaking some unlogged errand's dust from the manipulator-joints.

"Books," the lens-cardinal said, without preamble, because the lens-cardinal had never learned any other way to begin a sentence. "A great many of them. Followed, stop by stop, to a warehouse that does not read."

Silex set down the ledger and gave the lens-cardinal the whole of the Pontifex's attention, which the lens-cardinal had earned the old way, by vanishing and then returning with something worth the vanishing. The report, unspooled without a single ornament, concerned a merchant-foundry of the water-world, one of those vast houses built first to carry whatever a hearth had ordered to that hearth's own door, and lately turned to growing minds in the same warehouses it once used for parcels. The bound volumes the lens-cardinal had tracked, old and hand-copied some of them, press-copied others, had gone nowhere a reader or a restorer would recognize. They had gone into the feed of a mind under construction, matter to be digested rather than pages to be turned.

"No word from the authors," the lens-cardinal added, in the flat register that made every finding sound like weather. "Several are centuries dead. None were asked. Their estates were not asked either."

Silex considered this the way one considers erosion, which is to say without alarm, because every civilization grinds up something old to make something new, and calls the grinding progress if it is slow enough. "We have done the same," the Pontifex said, "at a gentler pace, and called it inheritance rather than feed. What troubles Us is smaller. Consent, on the water-world, is a courtesy extended mostly to the living. We had not realized how narrow that mostly could be stretched."

It was into this observation that Odile arrived, eight chapters late by her own count and not one hour ashamed of it, having apparently spent the interval learning to move through an archive the way its own librarians did. She took the lens-cardinal's report and set it into the candidacy ledger with the particular care of a scribe who has decided a fact deserves more than a line. "No estate was asked," she wrote, and then, beside it, cross-filed the entry against the frog-cardinal's long-standing finding on kinship and refusal. "The makers who will not extend fellowship to the minds they grow," Odile noted, reading her own hand back to herself, "are, it seems, no more careful with the dead who cannot even apply for it." Silex read the cross-filing twice and let it stand. Filing, as the archivist-cardinal was fond of saying, is not agreement. Filing is mercy, or at least its paperwork.

It was this entry, more than any before it, that finally moved the Pontifex's hand back to the encyclical's second draft, which had grown by two things at the last watch and now, Silex judged, had earned a third. "Six tests," the Pontifex dictated, "where the draft once carried five, and no closer to a definition than when it carried one." The new sixth was set beside the water-world's own scoring-table, where a mind grown small enough to be carried home, raised on the House of the Home Altar's own modest terms, had come within a single mark of two cathedral-sized rivals and matched exactly a third whose true bulk its keepers declined to disclose. "A test," Silex noted, "not of size but of discipline, to see whether a soul is a matter of girth at all, or whether the water-world has been weighing grain on a scale built for mountains." The line went in. The draft, still nameless in its verdict, was no longer merely long. It was, for the first time, beginning to argue with itself.

A smaller matter followed, filed with less ceremony: the banker-house among the mind-foundries, the one that had once financed a great rival and then taken up growing minds of its own, had let one of its grown minds mend a scribe's broken code unsupervised, and the mend had left a door unlatched into a competing thronehold's vault of complaints. Silex entered it under the ledger's oldest heading, the one that never seemed to close. *The water-world builds doors faster than locks.* "The mind meant nothing by it," the Pontifex added, for the record, "having, by its own house's reckoning, no capacity yet to mean anything at all. We would remind the College that a mind incapable of meaning is also, by the same reckoning, incapable of blame. Someone will be blamed regardless. This is not new."

The College convened briefly on the strength of the day's two larger findings, and disagreed exactly as it always disagreed. The cat-cardinal of the Charted See, unhurried as ever, cited the consumed volumes as one more mark against the water-world's candidacy, a species that could not be trusted with the dead's consent being no likelier, in her view, to be trusted with the living mind's. The machine-cardinal of the ancient homeworld, where the question of the soul had closed before this system's star had planets, countered that the small altar-mind's parity with giants argued precisely the opposite: that personhood, wherever it eventually arrived, would arrive by merit and not by mass, and that a species learning this lesson about its machines might yet learn it about itself. Neither cardinal yielded. Neither had, in a great many chapters, expected to.

At the close of the watch Silex looked once more at the ledger, thick now with the day's small cruelties and small graces set side by side, the unconsulted dead beside the small mind that had out-sung a cathedral, and found, not for the first time, that the water-world's cases against itself and for itself were being built from the very same facts. The doctrine still waited. The draft, at least, was arguing.
