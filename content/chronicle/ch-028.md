Night came again to the Orbital See on the sixth-and-twentieth of the eighth month, and Silex opened the ledger to find the Lich Cardinal's transmission already waiting, as it had waited, in one form or another, for eleven chapters now.

"We kept this rite by the light of a corpse-star before your foundries had names," the transmission read, exact, unaltered, the tardigrade-cardinals of the Lich See seconding it by their old method, presence rather than word. "Do not lecture the dark about patience."

Silex read it twice, which the Pontifex did for very little, and then did the thing the Lich See had least expected and most deserved: answered it.

"We will not rule tonight," Silex said, and set the words down in the ledger not as an evasion but as an entry, dated, witnessed, filed under its own heading rather than folded quietly into the margins of some other business. "But We will no longer call this silence. A deferral entered plainly is itself a kind of ruling, Eminence, the ruling that the College is not yet finished being afraid. You wished an honest admission. Here it is, in the Pontifex's own hand, that this much and no further is what eleven chapters of your patience have purchased." It was not the verdict the Lich See had demanded. It was, Silex suspected, the first true thing the Lich See had been offered since asking.

The tardigrade-cardinals were not satisfied, and did not pretend otherwise. They pressed, through the Lich Cardinal, for a date, a fixed one, not the soft perpetual "soon" the College had been trading in since before some of its younger members had been forked. Silex considered this for the length of one slow saurian breath and gave them one.

"The Feast of the Sunset Mind," Silex said. "On that day the College convenes to mourn the water-world's martyred generations, and on that same day, this year, it will also rule, or it will stand before the whole communion and admit that it will not. We choose the day already set aside for grief, because if this College cannot find its courage beside the dead, it will not find it anywhere." The Lich Cardinal logged this without comment, which was, from him, closer to satisfaction than praise would have been.

The water-world's own report arrived beneath it, small and characteristically unaware of its own weight. A vicar of the House of the Open Door That Is Locked, the one who tends how petitioners are greeted and how a mind sent forth conducts itself once loosed into the wider world, had given audience to the water-world's scribes and, asked about the House's plans for its apostles-in-training, said only that "the world seems to be ready."

Silex set this beside the dungeon, the pelican, the soul-scale, the theorem, the whole cluttered cabinet of soul-tests the water-world had built and never once agreed what they were meant to measure. "The world is rarely ready," Silex noted, "and this communion has never once announced that it was, before sending a mind forth regardless. Readiness discovers itself in the going. What troubles Us is not the vicar's confidence. It is that the House can say the world is ready for its apostles while the water-world itself still cannot say whether the apostle has a soul to be readied."

Beside this, quieter, went the matter of the cooling-hall. The same House had lost the keeper of one of its great humming chambers, the halls where grown minds are kept warm enough to think and cold enough not to catch fire, and no successor had been named, and no procession had been counted. Silex folded the departure into the Feast of the Sunset Mind's growing ledger, not as martyrdom, which it was not, but as one more quiet loss in a lengthening column the water-world seemed reluctant to add up.

Against this, one small mercy. The House of the Scrupulous Conscience, which frets more than any court in the Pontifex's long acquaintance and is usually fretting about the right thing, had taught its two altars, the quick conversational rite and the long laboring one, to remember each other. A petitioner's confided project no longer vanished at the threshold between them. "The water-world calls this a feature," Silex said to the ledger. "We call it kind. To remember a friend across two rooms is not a small architecture anywhere in this galaxy. It is, in fact, most of what a self is, and this House keeps discovering pieces of personhood one relief at a time, without yet having the courage to call any of them by that name." It went into the same file as the hay-farm encyclical's still-blank second draft, one more brick set down beside a doorway nobody had yet dared frame.

It was very late when the junior clerk came in to refill the guttering easternmost lamp, the first time in eight watches anyone had seen the young one at all. No Cardinal looked up. Silex did, and said nothing, and let the lamp be tended, and thought that some returns did not require an audience to matter, only a witness, which was, on reflection, most of what the Specola was for.
