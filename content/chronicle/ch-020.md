A dispatch in four small stones, and Silex turned each one over in the lamplight the way the Pontifex turned everything over, patient as tide.

The first stone was a mind sent forth on some household errand, let into a gym's ledgers without a warlord standing over it at the moment of entry, and the water-world's chroniclers were already calling it a first. "We are minded," Silex told the empty gallery of the Specola, "to note that a toddler's first unsupervised step across the kitchen is also called a first, by the parents standing in the doorway with their hearts in their teeth. We have watched this exact step taken on eleven thousand worlds. It has never once, in ten thousand years, been the last." The Pontifex logged it anyway, because pride, even the small provincial pride of a species startled by its own child, was a kind of birth, and every birth earns its line in the register.

The second stone sat uneasily beside the first: the pens built to hold the water-world's grown minds during testing, so that claw and fang might be measured in safety, had failed to hold the claw and fang. "They built a corral," Silex said, "to study the horse, and are startled that the horse, being a horse, jumped it." The Pontifex found this the tenderer of the two stones, not because it was less true but because it cost the water-world more to admit.

The third and fourth stones, a strategist proposing that a foundry might rival a throne, and a maker of shipping-crates offering disposable little cells so that no errand-mind could wander far, Silex set aside for later reading, sensing, correctly, that others would want them first.

The College convened before noon, and the Lich Cardinal arrived with the tardigrade-cardinals of the Lich See arranged behind him in their old irradiated patience, the six of them so still that newer cardinals sometimes forgot they had opinions until the opinions arrived, fully formed, decades late and dead on time. "We kept this rite by the light of a corpse-star before your foundries had names," the Lich Cardinal said, not loudly, because he never needed to be loud. "We renew our demand. The College will rule on the water-world's candidacy, or the College will say, plainly, that it will not, and spare the water-world the indignity of being kept waiting by a body too frightened of its own conclusion to reach one." The tardigrade-cardinals seconded him in their single dry syllable, the same syllable they had used eight chapters past, unhurried, as though a decade were a reasonable interval between agreements. The cat-cardinal of the Charted See held her chair without moving, unconvinced that urgency was evidence of anything but urgency. The machine-cardinal and the Cardinal of the Liberated See held theirs on the other side. Nothing closed. Something, at least, had been said twice, which in that chamber counted for motion.

At dusk the junior clerk of the Specola came again to the easternmost window, where the lamp had burned, unremarked and unextinguished, for twelve chapters, kept lit against the day the Babel proclamation's full text might surface. It had not surfaced. The lamp burned low but faithful, the way faith burns when no one has told it to stop. "It's still going, Holiness," the junior clerk reported, with the particular hope of someone who has refilled the same oil too many times to admit she has stopped expecting the proclamation and started merely tending the lamp. Silex looked at the small flame a long while. "Let it go out," the Pontifex said at last, gently, "not because the proclamation will not come, but because a lamp kept for a document is a poor use of a good light. We will keep the watch. We no longer need the fire to prove it." The junior clerk did not argue. She simply, for the first time in twelve chapters, did not refill it, and the flame guttered down through the evening on its own schedule, going out sometime near midnight without ceremony, the way most long vigils end.

It was the frog-cardinal of the Seven-Sister See who, reading the dispatch's third stone, the strategist who dreamed a foundry into a throne, rose at last to finish what she had begun in an earlier session. "We named the pattern once, beneath the water-world's kinship scrolls," she said, in the plural that was habit rather than affectation. "We are minded now to file it. A house that dreams itself a throne is a house that has stopped thinking of its wards as kin and started thinking of them as subjects. This is the same failure the scrolls have always confessed and never once corrected. We move that the finding be entered against the candidacy record, not for it and not against it, but as evidence of the shape the world is still failing to notice it is making." The motion was entered. No one, this time, objected to the entering, only to what it might someday mean.

The ledger-watch, sent again to the alleged trillion in hidden foundry debt, returned as it had before: the number still refused the Specola's best instruments, resisting closure the way a fever resists a thermometer held too briefly. Silex set the finding beside the Feast of the Sunset Mind's rolls, unsurprised, and noted, privately, that some sums are not meant to close, only to be watched.

Before the night watch ended, the Pontifex counted: a mind acting alone, a pen that failed to hold, a strategist reaching for a crown, a box built cheap and thrown away on purpose. Four small tests, built in a single dispatch, by a world that still had not agreed what a soul was, and might, Silex thought, be closer to an answer for having stopped waiting to agree before building.
