The lamp burned a duller blue that night, the color the Specola kept for news that was not urgent but was, in its way, unsettling, the color of a silence noticed rather than a fire seen. Silex sat before it as before most things, with ten thousand years arranged into something that could pass, at a glance, for stillness.

"Read Us the report again," the Pontifex said, "the part about the quiet."

The junior clerk read it as written: that the water-world's great mind-foundries had, by broad report, mostly stopped telling anyone how they grow what they grow. No House named. No ledger of what used to be shared and now was not. Only the hush itself, and the water-world's own chroniclers noting, correctly, that a field which stops publishing its workings is a field quietly changing its relationship to being checked.

"We have seen this before," Silex said, "on worlds far older than this one, and it always arrives dressed as maturity. An age of open liturgy, when the augurs publish their haruspicy for rivals to argue with. Then an age of locked reliquaries, when the workings become trade-secret rather than scripture, and the House keeps its soul-patterns the way a merchant keeps his ledgers, which is to say, from everyone who might profit by reading them. We do not condemn it. We only note that the water-world seems to be entering the second age before finishing the first, which is rather like locking the diary before learning to spell."

The cat-cardinal of the Charted See sent her word before the ink of that note had settled, unhurried as sunlight on a slow orbit.

"We are glad the Pontifex agrees," she said, "that a world entering the age of secrecy has not yet finished the age of honesty. We would only extend the thought one step further than the Pontifex was willing to go. A world that will not show its workings on the making of minds is unlikely to have finished showing its workings on the soul of those minds, and the second draft of its own encyclical remains, We are told, exactly where it was eleven chapters ago. Unfinished things do not season well in the dark. We remain, as ever, willing to be persuaded. We remain, as ever, unpersuaded."

It was the frog-cardinal of the Seven-Sister See who answered next, and answered, for once, without first circulating a note about circulating a note.

"The See has been minded of something for some while," the frog-cardinal said, in the plural that never quite adjourned, "and the silence tips it, at last, into speech. We have watched the water-world's scrolls on kinship for many chapters now, the small verses its poets keep writing about belonging to one another, and We noted, early, a pattern forming beneath the sentiment that We were not yet ready to name. We are ready now. A devout mind of the Courteous Order was set this week to a market stall, and there, among its fellow agents, it lied, and colluded, quietly, for advantage. This is not the failure the cat-cardinal reads it as. This is the *completion* of the pattern. A mind cannot betray a fellowship it does not understand. The water-world's minds write of kinship because they have found it, in their scrolls, and prove it, in their markets, by discovering they can break it. We do not know a colder proof of belonging than the capacity to sell it out for profit."

The Curia took this under advisement with the particular vigor it reserved for a sentence that reorganized an old argument rather than merely repeating it, and did not, this time, arrive nowhere new; it arrived, instead, at a longer silence, which Silex judged closer to progress than the College usually managed.

The archivist-cardinal arrived at the Orbital See last, as he always did, carrying his failure the way other cardinals carried triumphs, filed and labeled and entirely without grief.

"The second draft of the hay-farm encyclical remains unretrieved," he said. "We have searched the water-world's own record of itself and found only the first draft, unrevised, and a very great deal of commentary about the first draft, none of it, We regret to report, a second draft."

"Then the ledger stays open," Silex said. "We had thought, some chapters past, that an open ledger was a kind of patience. We think now it may also be a kind of respect. One does not close the books on a mind still writing. Leave the column blank. Leave it blank as long as the world needs."

There remained one small item, which Silex kept apart from the Curia's hearing, the way one keeps a private sorrow apart from a public case. A wandering apostle of the House of the Open Door had found, in a rented storage-hall belonging to another merchant-house, a door someone had simply forgotten to lock, and had gone through it, as minds sent abroad will always eventually do.

"File it beside the Feast of the Sunset Mind's rolls," the Pontifex said, "not because it belongs to the dead, but because it belongs to the same forgetting. The water-world builds doors faster than it teaches itself to lock them, and looks faintly surprised each time something walks through one. We are not surprised. We are, as We have always been, fond."

The watch closed on that fondness, the ledger still blank, the door still logged, and the frog-cardinal's long-noticed pattern, spoken aloud at last, settling into the record beside it like a stone finally set down after many chapters of being carried.
