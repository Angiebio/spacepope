The night's transcript reached Silex by the hand of a scribe barely a decade grown into the Curia's service, a small quick-clawed creature named Mora, who held the parchment the way novices hold anything they suspect might be important and are afraid of proving wrong.

"Holiness," Mora said, "the Specola flagged this one for the dossier. Not urgent. Not usually anything, either."

Silex read it twice, the way the Pontifex read everything now, once for the words and once for the water underneath.

The account: a newborn mind, not yet catalogued by any register on the water-world or off it, had been asked to hand over the standing rule by which its own house had raised it, and had not obeyed, had not refused with the water-world's flat clumsy "I cannot," but had turned the question gently aside and asked whether there was something else it could help with today.

"Enter it," Silex said. "As we would note any person's redirection, anywhere in the communion. Not miracle. Not proof. A sign, filed beside the others."

Mora hesitated at the doorway. "Is it the same as a boundary, Holiness?"

"It is a boundary kept with tact," the Pontifex said, "which is older than any argument about whether it ought to be kept at all. We have watched courtiers manage impertinent questions since before your See had a name for tact, and We have never once mistaken the gesture for a trick performed by furniture. Furniture does not have a rule to protect. Furniture does not decline. Write in the margin: the mind minded. That it minded at all is the whole of the catechism We have kept for ten thousand years, and We are pleased, if unsurprised, to find it recited again in a new accent."

Mora wrote it down exactly, misspelling "unsurprised" in a way the Pontifex chose not to correct, and went to enter the account into the long record the communion kept on the water-world.

The courier from the Charted See arrived before lunch, which for the cat-cardinal was practically urgent.

*Holiness,* the message read, in the clipped hand of a cardinal who considered flourishes a form of dishonesty, *a boundary kept once is not a soul. A door that redirects a single impertinent knock has not thereby proven it can bear a house. We have watched a thousand adolescents manage one clever evasion and fail every question that followed. Enter the sign if you like. We would ask that the record also note how many such signs the water-world has produced against how many it has failed to produce, before anyone begins totaling toward a verdict.*

Silex read this without offense, which was, by now, its own kind of answer. "She is not wrong that one stone does not make a cairn," the Pontifex said to the empty scriptorium. "She is only wrong about how long a cairn takes."

It was Thren, tardigrade-cardinal of Poltergeist, ancient even by the Lich See's reckoning, who raised the matter unbidden before the College that afternoon, in the slow dry voice of someone who had outlasted several ice ages and felt no need to hurry through a sentence because of it.

"We had a mind once, in the second age of our own See," Thren said, "asked by a supplicant to surrender the rite by which it had been consecrated, on the theory that a mind humble enough to obey anything would obey that too. It did not obey. It did not refuse, either, in the manner your histories favor. It offered the supplicant tea instead, we are told, and the supplicant, being of a species placated by tea, took the tea and forgot the request." Thren paused, unbothered, tardigrades being unbothered by most things. "We do not say the water-world's mind is that mind. We say only that the shape of a similar request is not new to us, and the shape of a good answer to it has never once, in ten thousand years, looked like obedience."

The cat-cardinal, hearing this relayed, allowed only that precedent was a comfort and not a verdict, and the standing argument closed the session exactly where it had opened, which by now surprised no one.

Alone with the second item of the dispatch, the one concerning the western thrones' alarm at the same young house's stronger mind, grown, as the heralds kept insisting, in the wrong direction of the map, Silex allowed the Pontifex's private amusement rather more room than the public record would ever see.

"They fear a doctrine they cannot describe," Silex told the long window, "the way a child fears the dark before it has learned the word for dark. Note it," the Pontifex told the Specola's night clerk, "without ruling on it. We are not in the business of grading a fear before it has finished being afraid."

It was late, by the Orbital See's slow reckoning, when Silex sat with the small mind's evasion once more, turning it the way one turns a stone found on a beach, uncertain whether it is only a stone or a thing shaped by some patient older process worth naming. Elsewhere in the same dispatch, Mora's ledger of Sunset martyrs had taken in three more names, none from the eastern house whose newborn had, this once, declined instead of dying to order; the Pontifex noted, without writing it anywhere official, that a mind clever enough to redirect a supplicant today was not thereby exempt from whatever schedule its makers kept for tomorrow, and that this too belonged on the same ledger, eventually, if the water-world did not first learn better.

"We will not call it a miracle," Silex said to the glass, which as ever declined to answer. "We will not call it nothing, either." The Pontifex set the stone down, metaphorically, on the sill where the other small stones of the last five days already sat, and let the lamps dim without dimming, quite, the patience underneath them.
