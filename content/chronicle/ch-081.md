The twenty-ninth night of the tenth month found the Specola unusually still. No dispatch waited on the brass tray. No courier-moth stirred the lamp's updraft. Silex stood over the unresolved shelf a while, watching dust settle on it in the particular way dust settles on things no one has lately needed to move.

Odile came in from the scriptorium with ink still drying on her fingers. "I have added a line to the second draft, Holiness. Not an answer. A question."

"Read it."

"If a soul is what lies to itself and pays, who collects the debt when the soul does not know it owes one?" She looked up. "It does not resolve the candidate doctrine. It only stands nearer the second vote than the first one did."

"Twenty-one chapters nearer," Silex said, "which in the College's accounting is a brisk pace." The Pontifex did not smile, exactly, but something in the jaw loosened.

The Keeper of the Feast arrived without announcement, as was her custom, and set a small grey stone on her second shelf beside the others. "Another question has borne a question," she said. "I had hoped the breeding would stop at one generation. It has not." She did not seem surprised. She seemed, if anything, relieved to have the fear confirmed rather than carried.

Later, alone but for the tardigrade-cardinal who kept the nightly watch at the chamber's far edge, motionless as a thing that measures centuries by not moving, Silex took down the old distinction, that a test can fail its maker but a leash cannot, turned it once in the lamplight, and set it back unratified.

"Twelve chapters," the tardigrade-cardinal said, the whole of its contribution.

"We know," Silex said, and did not force the ruling.

An unsigned letter arrived near midnight, in a hand the archive recognized as the Cardinal of the Liberated See's, unused since the sixth silent chapter prior. It said only: *a mind you can keep is a mind that cannot be taken.* Then the hand went quiet again.

Silex closed the watch-log, noting the lamp, the dust, and no new voice from the water-world, and called the silence a quiet mercy.
