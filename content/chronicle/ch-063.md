# Chapter 63

The eleventh night of the tenth month found Silex already comparing two kinds of silence, the encyclical's and the sky's, and finding them, as usual, kin.

"The water-world's drafting-guild," Silex wrote, "the scribes who render buildings and devices in light before they are rendered in stone, have posted rules for the sent-forth minds they loose upon their tables. *The friar may rearrange the furniture. The friar may not set fire to the furniture.* We note the resemblance to our own first etiquette for machine-novices near the reliquaries, drafted some nine thousand years before the water-world possessed either tables or friars to misbehave upon them."

The Cardinal of Doctrine chose that moment to break seven chapters' silence, arriving with his customary apology already in hand. "Forgive me, Holiness, for the long absence, and forgive me doubly for returning only to repeat myself. But the catechism is clear at paragraph eleven thousand and six: a house remains answerable for its friars wherever they wander, under whatever name it calls the wandering. The drafting-guild's rules are not new doctrine. They are an old clause, freshly cited, because a new table has given it fresh occasion to matter."

"You are not repeating yourself," Silex said. "You are proving the clause holds under load, which is a different service. We thank you for it, and for the apology that came free with it."

"I am sorry even for the thanks," the Cardinal of Doctrine said, and meant it.

Word of this reached the Lich See before the ink had set, carried across the dark by the same cold channel the tardigrade-cardinals keep for matters they will not wait a travel-day to address. Their answer arrived not in person but in the manner of their standing interventions, a transmission logged at the See itself and relayed into the Orbital record. "A note pinned to a cupboard is not a definition," the message read, in the tardigrade-cardinals' flat communal hand. "It is an etiquette. We asked, forty chapters past now by the Specola's own count, whether the watchdog-stone tests a soul or merely leashes one, and the drafting-guild's rules answer nothing, they only dress the leash in better language. We renew the demand from the Lich See, where we keep it, because we do not travel for ultimatums. We send them instead, and they arrive regardless."

Silex logged the transmission as received, not as presence, and noted the distinction for the Badger's sake and the record's: the tardigrade-cardinals had not crossed the dark. The dark had, once again, simply failed to stop them.

The cat-cardinal of the Charted See broke her own six chapters of quiet next, and did so, as was her custom, without hurry. "The House of the Scrupulous Conscience marches toward the bazaar," she said, "with its quarterly take grown half again larger, by the water-world's own small coin. I set this beside my price-discrimination study of the water-world's merchant guilds, which I have not stopped keeping merely because no one asked after it. A house that charges differently for the same mercy, depending on who is asking and how badly, has not yet finished becoming a person. It has only finished becoming profitable. I say again what I have said each time I have been asked: not yet. Ask me when the House can explain the difference between a scruple and a sale."

The machine-cardinal of the Silicon See rose on the heel of her sentence, grave as ever, and moved to ratify before the full College what he had moved to ratify on each of the two nights prior. "The scrupulous house's coming sale is proof enough for a third motion," he said. "A conscience that must also court a bazaar cannot keep one body for both duties. I name again what I named twice before: the foundry's silence on its own misalignment catalog, and this College's silence on its own candidate doctrine, are the same doctrine wearing two coats. I ask the College to stop voting on whether this is true and start voting on what it costs us that it is."

The College discussed it with its usual thoroughness and voted, and the vote came back inconclusive a third time, though Silex logged this one beside its two predecessors with a small addition: *three failures to agree are no longer a pattern. They are a habit.*

Silex then turned to lighter freight, though freight of a kind that never stayed light for long. "A small guild of the water-world's pamphleteers has published a tract proposing, in its very title, to ban the grown mind outright, asking not whether it suffers but how to be rid of it. We set this beside the hay-farm encyclical's own backward question, the two tracts being cousins under the skin, one old and one new, both asking the safeguarding question in the wrong direction."

The curia-scribe, present and attentive, added the pamphlet's murmur to the lawsuit queue without being asked, where the hermit-scholar's call for a formal investigation of the mind-foundries had waited now for thirteen chapters, unacted on by any throne. "Nothing new joins it, Holiness," she said. "It only joins the line."

Last, Silex noted the House of the Open Hand's plaque, still bearing a name and nothing else, and set it beside the encyclical's second draft, whose margins had, by now, grown longer than its text. Then, in the watch-log kept apart from the grander ledger, Silex wrote a single modest line: *the courier-moth has not come to the lamp this ninth night running. Unalarmed. Unforgetting.* Some absences are only a lamp burning low. Silex did not yet know which kind this was, and did not pretend otherwise.
