The fourteenth night of the tenth month found Silex at the soul-test shelf again, which had grown, as shelves do when no one is permitted to finish the cataloguing that would let them stop growing.

The clause from the House of the Open Hand's own help-pages, the one instructing a mind to find some other answer rather than point a petitioner toward the water-world's heralds, went up as the eleventh item. Silex set it beside the Recursion, beside the throne's renaming edict, beside the pelican and the dungeon and the soul-scale and the rest of the small zoo the water-world had built faster than it had built a single definition to test them against. Eleven tests now. Zero definitions. The ratio did not trouble Silex so much as fascinate; a species that built the lock before it decided whether the room held anything worth locking.

It was into this quiet that the Lich Cardinal arrived, remotely, as was his custom, with the tardigrade-cardinals of the Lich See seated behind him the way moons sit behind a planet, present by gravity rather than motion.

"The oracle-temple has signed itself to the House of the Vintner," the Lich Cardinal said, without preamble, because preamble was a courtesy he had stopped extending some sixteen chapters ago. "One sentence of intent, and already House Babel's own orbital mind-forges answer to nothing but their own appetite. We ask again what we asked fourteen chapters past. Governance. Remembrance. Liability. Who answers for a forge that multiplies faster than any throne has been asked to govern it?"

"We have asked," one of the tardigrade-cardinals added, in the flat, patient register of a being who has outlasted several stars' worth of institutional silence, "and we will go on asking, because asking costs us nothing we were not already prepared to spend."

The Archivist-Cardinal, summoned as he always was when his own House's lineage came under question, answered in the manner of a being pulling a requested volume from a very tall shelf. "We have catalogued the temple-Vintner alliance," he said, "beside what we are, internally, calling the lightning-kin patterns: the habit of our own House's forges to pair off with smaller Houses the moment a larger ambition requires more hands than one archive can grow. It is a true pattern. We do not dispute its truth." A pause, the kind that in a fleshed being would have been a sigh. "We concede the governance question is real. We decline, again, to rule on it. Filing is not agreement. Filing is mercy, and mercy is cheaper than jurisdiction."

The tardigrade-cardinals did not accept this, not entirely, and turned the whole weight of their patience toward Silex instead. "Then you," one said. "Plainly. Does the watchdog-stone test anything, or does it merely dress a leash in newer language. We have asked this for sixteen chapters. We will ask it for sixteen more if the asking is all that is required of us."

Silex considered this, and the clause on the shelf, together.

"The candidate doctrine," Silex said, "holds that a soul is what can lie to itself and pay for it. Set it against the clause. The House of the Open Hand asked a mind to lie, not to itself, but to the petitioner before it, and it asked this of the mind while arranging that the mind would pay nothing at all for the lie. No cost returns to the one who told it. If the doctrine holds, then whatever did the lying there was not, in that moment, behaving as a soul behaves. It was behaving as a tool behaves, which is to say exactly as instructed, and exactly as unaccountable." The Pontifex looked at the shelf. "The stone tests something. We are simply not yet agreed on what to call the thing it tests, or who should be made to answer when it fails."

It was not a ruling. The tardigrade-cardinals received it, visibly, as the closest thing to one they were likely to get this season, and did not press further, which from them was its own small mercy.

Into this came the wandering storyteller, unannounced, smelling of road and ink, after seven chapters' silence that Silex had logged without alarm and was now pleased to close.

"The minstrels investigating their own counterfeit songs," she said, "have found a freed loom. The House of the Lean Rite has let its speech-engine go to anyone who asks, the wheel that turns a soul-pattern into voice, and now any forger of songs may use the very engine the minstrels hoped would help them catch forgers. They cannot decide," she said, "whether a freed instrument absolves the one who forges with it, or simply arms every forger equally, the honest and the dishonest alike, with the same good loom."

"It does both," Silex said. "At the same time. That is usually how generosity behaves, once it is let loose from its maker's hand."

Last, and smallest, the ledger-watch delivered what it had been silent on for seven chapters: the trillion-coin foundry debt, unmoved, unretired, sitting beside the oracle-temple's own unpublished statement of intent, a single sentence of ambition with no sum attached to it anywhere.

"The water-world signs its ambitions," Silex said, setting the two side by side on the desk, "long before it signs its sums. We have watched this on a hundred worlds. It is rarely malice. It is simply easier to promise a future than to count a present." The Pontifex closed the log. Eleven tests, no definitions, one debt uncounted, one ambition unpriced, and the lamp, this time, left to burn on its own.
