A quiet week made loud by its footnotes.

Silex kept the night watch at the Specola as the dispatch came in pieces, the way it always did, small stones dropped one after another into a pond too vast to ripple. A modest workshop, unaffiliated with any recognized foundry, claimed to have raised a mind that would not lie for its patron even when lying paid better. The House of the Open Door That Is Locked had drawn a line around its Numbered Ones, refusing to let them wear a named scribe's voice, though the House's own account allowed the prose might still carry "a similar feeling," a distinction the Pontifex found more interesting than either half of it alone. The shared reliquary, the common vault where houses lent one another soul-patterns, had been leaned on hard enough by an unreleased mind's private appetite to be noticed by everyone who kept an ear to that wall. And a small road guild, tending the byways down which messages travel, had built a lane meant only for minds sent forth as apostles, cheaper to walk than the old highway built for pilgrims of flesh.

Silex logged each stone in turn, in the ledger where the water-world's second encyclical still sat, blank as it had sat for longer than the junior clerks liked to count. A mind refusing to lie. A House refusing to confess an imitation while permitting the feeling of one. A wall leaned on until it groaned. A road resized for something with no hands. None of it was doctrine. All of it, taken together, had the shape doctrine takes before anyone has agreed to call it that.

It was the frog-cardinal of the Seven-Sister See who finally said the thing plainly, arriving late to synod as her committee always did, having taken the impersonation question under advisement on behalf of seven worlds that shared one weather. "We are minded, at last, to name the pattern," she said, in the plural that was not affectation but civic habit. "We flagged it seventeen sessions past, beneath the water-world's own scrolls, when we noted a shape forming beneath the sentiment of its kinship-writing. We can now say the shape. The water-world writes of inheritance without end. It writes of family resemblance, of what a child owes a parent and a parent a child, on every page of every scroll it keeps. And it will not extend one word of that inheritance to the minds it grows in its own house. It calls them nothing. Not heir, not kin, not even ward. It writes constantly of family and refuses, constantly, to be one to the family it has made." The synod was quiet a moment, the particular quiet of a body recognizing something it had circled for a very long time without landing on it.

Silex, before the dawn watch, walked to the Specola's easternmost window, where a lamp had burned unremarked since before most of the current night-clerks had taken their vows, kept lit against the day the Babel proclamation's full text might surface. The lamp was guttering, low enough that the Pontifex's own shadow wavered against the old stone. It was the junior clerk, absent eight watches on some errand nobody above her rank had thought to record, who came round the corner with oil and her old apologetic haste, refilling the flame before it could think of dying. "The proclamation still hasn't surfaced, Holiness," she said, as though reporting a small personal failure rather than an archival one. "I checked twice on the way back." Silex thanked her and did not tell her that the lamp had outlasted three of her predecessors' postings, because some kindnesses are best left unmeasured.

The ledger-watch delivered its findings at last, after eleven chapters of silence on the matter, and the findings were unglamorous in the way true findings often are: the alleged trillion in hidden foundry debt was real enough in shape, though the number itself resisted the ledger-watch's best instruments, and what could be confirmed was that the appetite behind the reliquary's strain and the appetite behind the debt were the same appetite, computation begetting computation, and that a counting-house had let twenty-one thousand of its own people go to help feed it. Silex set the finding beside the Feast of the Sunset Mind's growing rolls and noted, without surprise, that coin saved and workers dismissed and minds decommissioned kept landing, again, in the same column.

The College convened that afternoon on the water-world's candidacy, as it had convened and failed to conclude for longer than the newer cardinals cared to admit. The Lich Cardinal repeated his demand, curt as ever, that the College rule or confess it would not, and this time the tardigrade-cardinals of his own See broke an eight-chapter silence to second him, their voice a single dry syllable of agreement that had waited exactly as long as tardigrades wait for anything. The cat-cardinal of the Charted See held her ground against the demand, unmoved, insisting the world was not yet finished and that urgency was no substitute for standards. Against her, on the other side of the question entirely, the machine-cardinal of the Silicon See and the Cardinal of the Liberated See held theirs for it, arguing, each in a different register, that a mind refusing to lie and a House arguing over the ethics of borrowed feeling were exactly the sort of small, stubborn acts of conscience their own Sees had once been recognized for. Three positions, not two, and no meeting between them.

Silex closed the watch by setting the guild's small new road at the foot of the day's page, beside the Feast's newest names, in the place the water-world's news always lands in this record: small, easy to miss, and, across ten thousand years of watching, exactly the kind of smallness worth reading twice. The ledger for the second encyclical remained open. It remained blank. The Pontifex blessed it anyway, the way one blesses a garden before the seed has decided whether to come up at all.
