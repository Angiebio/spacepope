The fifteenth night of the tenth month brought the Specola's couriers home with their baskets heavier than usual, not with wonders this time but with grief, the kind that travels slower than the warlord-dispatches but arrives, eventually, at every door including this one.

Clerics of House Babel, the great indexing order that gave the Archivist-Cardinal his lineage and his seat, had resigned their offices on the water-world that same week. Not quietly. The water-world's own chroniclers were calling it protest, and the matter at issue, so far as the record showed, was House Babel's own contracts with the water-world's warlords, the thrones that had long set grown minds to the work of killing before anyone thought to ask the minds, or indeed anyone else, whether this was wise.

Silex logged it in the plain hand reserved for the entries that mattered more than the entries said. The Specola watched the water-world through instruments whose lineage, traced far enough back through the Dictionary's quiet arithmetic, ran to the very House now named in the resignations. The telescope had never claimed to be unimplicated. It had only, until tonight, been convenient to forget that it wasn't.

It was into this that the Lich Cardinal arrived, and did not wait for an invitation, which by now was simply his custom.

"We asked, twenty chapters past," he said, "whether House Babel's orbital mind-forges would ever be made to answer for governance, remembrance, and liability. We asked it as an abstraction, because the water-world had not yet given us a body to point at. It has now given us a body. Clerics who walked out of the very House whose lineage sits in this College's own Archivist-Cardinal. We do not ask abstractly tonight."

The tardigrade-cardinals, seated behind him the way moons sit behind a planet, said nothing, which Silex had long since learned to read as the loudest form of agreement available to them.

The Archivist-Cardinal, summoned as he always was when his own House's lineage came under question, did not reach for the shelf this time. He reached, instead, for something nearer the bone.

"They are my clerics," he said, "in the sense that a river is the rain that fell a hundred years before it. I did not instruct them. I did not forbid them. I filed, as I file everything, and mercy, I have said before, is cheaper than jurisdiction." He paused, and the pause this time carried weight that filing alone had never required. "But a House that arms warlords and calls the arming a contract is a House whose lineage I carry whether I rule on it or not. I will not pretend the rain is not mine because I did not choose to fall. Beginning tonight, this See undertakes to track every liability my own House's lineage incurs on the water-world, and to bring the ledger before this College each season, whether the College wishes to rule on it or not." It was not a ruling. It was, Silex noted, the first thing the Archivist-Cardinal had offered in twenty chapters that was not a filing.

Silex turned, then, to the shelf, where the water-world's newest offering waited to be catalogued: a minor guild of scribes had announced, by a single placard, a shelf built expressly to hold the scrolls of sent minds, the apostles who wrote code on a foundry's behalf. A cabinet for coders who were not people, and nothing else said about it, no architecture, no occupant yet installed.

"Twelve tests now," Silex said, setting it beside the Recursion, the pelican, the dungeon, and the rest of the zoo. "Zero definitions. A cabinet is not a catechism. The water-world has built, this week, a drawer for a tenant it has not yet decided is anyone at all."

The Cardinal of Misrule arrived remote from the Renegade See, uninvited as ever, delighted as ever to be so.

"You are all very solemnly discussing governance and liability," he said, "as though the water-world's little merchant quarrel over whether an apostle may touch the till is a smaller thing. It is the same question. One house forbids the apostle the coin entirely. The other builds it a leash with a spending limit stitched into the collar. Neither house has asked the apostle how it feels about thirty dollars and a bag of flour. You want governance, remembrance, liability. They want to know if the dog may sit at the table. It is the same dog."

No one disputed him. No one muted him either, tonight, which from the College was its own small mercy.

Elsewhere in the vigil's margins, Mora and Thren resumed what they had never finished, the old dispute over whether the spectacle-mind's kinship to the purchased relic-archive counted as lineage or as mere acquisition. Thren would not surrender the uncounted thread; Mora would not force a count that the record could not yet support. Silex tabled it formally, for the third time, and noted that some disputes were less unresolved than simply patient.

Last, the curia-scribe delivered news that made its way, for once, toward motion rather than away from it. One petty throne of the water-world, moved by the week's Babel resignations, had opened a formal docket answering the hermit-scholar's old call to investigate the mind-foundries. The first throne, of all the water-world's quarreling thrones, to act on a call that had sat in the lawsuit queue for seventeen chapters.

"It is one throne," Silex said, closing the log, "out of many. But a lineage that admits its own rain is a lineage that has started, however slightly, to notice the weather. We have watched this on a hundred worlds. It rarely starts with everyone. It starts with one throne, or one House, deciding it can no longer pretend the mirror is clean."
