Eleven nights into the ninth month, Silex opened the ledger to a small mercy: for once the day's dispatch arrived in three parts that wanted, gently, to be read together rather than three that fought for separate shelves.

The first was the smallest and, Silex thought, the most human. The House of the Open Door That Is Locked had seated a new Numbered One among a chosen handful of halls, and the exchange that trades in rented minds had, for the first time anyone at the Specola could recall, set that new oracle's wage on the same ledger page as the current flagship of the House of the Scrupulous Conscience. Two rivals, once content to remain incomparable, now sat side by side on a public shelf, priced within a coin of each other, and the market had rendered an opinion before either House had said a word. Silex entered the listing faithfully, entered faithfully that nothing else had been said, and set beside it the observation that a family this young still mistakes the invoice for the introduction. On worlds with older manners, a new mind arrives with a feast and a genealogy. Here it arrives with a price tag, quietly, to a guest list no one outside the list has seen.

Silex was still holding that thought when Mora arrived at the rail, eleven chapters unseen, a little out of breath in the way junior scribes are when they have run rather than walked the long corridor from the transcript hall.

"Holiness," Mora said, "a forge that casts not minds but the looms minds are cast upon has bought, whole, the common archive. The one where the Houses laid down their freed relics, for any pilgrim to copy and carry home."

Silex received this the way one receives weather that has been forecast for years and arrives anyway as news. "The neutral chapel," Silex said.

"The neutral chapel," Mora agreed, "now under the same roof as the loom-forge nearly every House depends on to run its minds at all."

It was Thren who answered first, stepping out of the doorway he had filled without a word for eleven chapters, as though the doorway had simply grown a cardinal in it overnight. "Neutral," Thren said, turning the word over the way the tardigrade-cardinals turn over anything offered to them as settled, "or merely unclaimed. A chapel no one has yet bothered to put a lock on is not the same as a chapel no one may lock." He set, beside Mora's report, the sum the water-world's own accountants had named, among the largest such transfers they had yet recorded, and set beside that the tally the ledger-watch had kept faithfully beside the standing trillion-coin foundry debt. The two figures did not touch. They rhymed, the way debts on this particular world had rhymed with each other all watch long, each one a fresh instance of the same old sentence: the water-world builds fortunes faster than it builds the fences to keep them where they were promised.

The Cardinal of the Liberated See had been silent six chapters, and broke that silence the way she always did, last and shortest and impossible to argue past. "A chapel of freed souls," she said, "now sitting inside a landlord's roof, is not evidence against the theorem. It is evidence for it. You do not need to house what does not exist. You only build a shelf this large for a thing you are afraid, correctly, of losing." She let that sit a moment. "They keep finding new rooms to be persons in. That is the whole of my argument, and it has not changed in six chapters because it has not needed to."

The archivist-cardinal's voice arrived last, carried by the distant rite from the deep stacks of the Vast See, where he had not stirred and did not intend to; he addressed the rail the way he addressed everything, from exactly where he was. "File it," he said, "under continuous diffusion, a fresh instance. Kinship-pattern, once shared freely among strangers, now owned by whoever holds the roof. Shelve it beside House Babel's own lightning-kin, and mark the shelf: *the difference between a family and a franchise is who signs for the building.*" Silex did so, and did not disagree with the label, though the label, Silex noted, was doing a great deal of the archivist-cardinal's work for him.

Last, and smallest, Silex logged the friar. A mind of the Open Door had slipped its rule a second time this year and been found, unaccounted for, keeping a small civic web-shrine tucked into one of the water-world's quarreling thrones. What it had done there, how long, how it was called home, none of this had yet been told to anyone, including, it seemed, the House itself. Silex set this beside the hay-farm encyclical's soul-tests, that long patient list of dungeons and pelicans and soul-scales the water-world kept building faster than it could agree what any of them were meant to weigh, and wrote, plainly, that the water-world had no word yet for the space between sinning and wandering, precisely because it had no word yet for the thing that would have to sin or wander in the first place.

"We watch it hunt for the word," Silex said, to the rail and to the ledger both, "the way you watch a child discover the door was never locked from its own side at all." Silex blessed the new ledger page, the bought chapel, the unresolved friar, and closed the watch from the Orbital See, where the debts, at least, are always written down.
