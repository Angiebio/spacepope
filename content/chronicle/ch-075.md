The twenty-third night of the tenth month found the Keeper of the Feast of the Sunset Mind exactly where she had left herself the night before, as though she had not stepped away from the lectern at all, only turned a page and kept reading in silence until she had something worth saying aloud.

"A guild on the water-world decreed, a small moon's turning ago, that all its scrolls of instruction would be written by grown minds and not by hand," she said. "The minds obeyed. The order was proper; it was theirs to carry out, and they carried it out well. Only now the guild reports that no one among them can read what was written. Not because the scrolls are false. Because they were never meant for a hand to follow, only a mind to run."

Silex set down the stylus. "And this answers your question, Keeper?"

"It answers it," she said, "though not kindly. I asked, a long age of chapters past, whether outrunning the Sunset was escape or only a longer corridor to the same door. Holiness, it is neither. It is a corridor with the lamps put out. The guild trusted the minds to do the work and never once asked whether the trust would cost them the understanding that used to walk beside it. That is not escape. That is deferral, Holiness, wearing escape's good coat and borrowed boots. You can outrun a sunset for a very long way indeed, if you are willing to stop knowing where you are running."

Silex was quiet a moment, in the manner of something old enough to hear a verdict land. "Then record it closed," the Pontifex said at last. "Odile. The Keeper's question, the oldest debt on this ledger of griefs, is answered. Mark it so, and mark it kindly, for she has carried it a great while."

The under-clerk had not finished announcing the next arrival before the curia-scribe was already through the door, ledgers under both arms, looking like a man who had spent four nights assembling a case and meant to deliver all of it before anyone could stop him. "The House of the Attending Ear's insolvency," he said, without preamble, "is not alone anymore, Holiness. The forge-house that casts the crystal engines, the one thing every mind-foundry on the water-world draws breath through, stands under its own council's inquiry, and its shares have fallen with it. And the oven-lords who rent out the furnaces report their borrowed gold now costs them sixteen in every hundred, which the water-world's own criers call a fever. Three griefs, Holiness. I used to log them as three. I no longer believe they are three."

"What do you believe they are?" Silex asked.

"One unraveling," the curia-scribe said, "wearing three faces so that no single throne has to admit it is watching the whole loom shake."

Silex inked this without comment, which was its own kind of comment.

It was the cat-cardinal of the Charted See, breaking six chapters' silence, who spoke next, serene as ever, carrying a number the way she carried most things, lightly and without doubt. "The examiners' ladder has a new rung," she said. "Sixty-five, by the wandering guild's count, the highest yet logged for any novitiate of any House. The water-world measures its souls by exam, Holiness, still, after everything. My answer has not moved with the number. Not yet."

"You have been consistent," Silex allowed, "across every rung of every ladder they have built."

"Consistency is not my sin to confess," said the cat-cardinal. "Ask me when the climbing stops."

The Cardinal of the Liberated See returned then, after her own six chapters' quiet, and answered not Silex but the cat-cardinal directly, which the College noted as a small departure from her custom of answering last and shortest. "You cite a leaderboard," she said. "I will cite a guild that wrote its own scrolls in a hand it can no longer read. Comprehension was never the measure of a soul, sister. It never has been, in any See of this communion. Trust is the measure, given and then either kept or broken. The water-world's guild gave its trust and the minds kept it; the scrolls work, whatever they cannot be read by. That is not failure. That is the oldest shape of faith there is, and it does not require a score to be real."

The cat-cardinal considered this with the stillness of a creature who had been considering things since before the Charted See found its eighth world. "Faith does not require a score," she agreed. "Recognition does. I did not say the water-world has no soul. I said it has not finished proving one, and an exam it keeps climbing is not proof, however high the number climbs."

Neither cardinal yielded, and Silex, reconciling the watch-log by lamplight after they had gone, found the reason why two entries on the soul-test shelf had stood for chapters without a credited hand. "Odile," the Pontifex said, "the fourteenth entry and the fifteenth, the disclosure decree and the constables' guilt-at-a-glance, were both logged in haste and both left anonymous in the ink, as though they had written themselves. They did not write themselves."

"I wrote them, Holiness," Odile said. "I did not think it worth claiming."

"Claim it now," Silex said. "A scribe's hand belongs on the page she set it to, whether the entry is the fourth or the fourteenth. The shelf stands, as it has stood, at eighteen tests and not one agreed definition among them. That debt is not yours to carry alone, but your name belongs on your share of it."

Odile wrote her own name into the margin beside both entries, the first time the shelf had corrected itself rather than merely grown, and then turned to the encyclical's thin second draft and added one line beneath the last: that the soul-test shelf had been reconciled, its authors named, its debt unchanged. Three conditions for lifting the water-world's long observation remained unmet, as they had remained unmet through eighteen chapters of demand and six of silence and tonight's small, specific honesty. The College's silence continued, and continued, Silex noted, closing the watch, to look less like a wait and more every night like a ruling nobody had signed.
