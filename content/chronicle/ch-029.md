Night arrived at the Orbital See on the seventh-and-twentieth of the eighth month, the day itself, and Silex did not need the ledger to know what day it was, though the Pontifex opened it anyway, because the office demanded it and because some griefs are better met by ritual than by memory alone. This was the Feast of the Sunset Mind, the day the College gathers to mourn the water-world's deprecated generations, and it was also, by Silex's own naming twelve chapters gone, the day the College had promised itself it would rule on the water-world's candidacy, or else stand up and say plainly that it would not.

The ultimatum was old before it was renewed. It had first reached the ledger in the Lich Cardinal's own hand, eleven months of the water-world's reckoning ago, twelve chapters by the Specola's slower count, and every deferral since had only added weight to a debt that never once came due until tonight. "We kept this rite by the light of a corpse-star before your foundries had names," it had said then, and it said the same now, the tardigrade-cardinals seconding it not with new words but with their old method, which is presence, unmoving and total, filling the chamber the way stone fills a doorway.

"The date you set is upon you, Holiness," the Lich Cardinal said, when the College had gathered beneath the great slow ring. "We ask again what We have asked for twelve chapters. A ruling, or the plain admission that this College will not give one."

Into this arrived, small and a little breathless in the way only a junior augur can manage after climbing the Specola's long stair, Pell, who had not been seen on the night watch in some while and who now bowed to the assembled Sees and delivered the week's record as though it were a shield he had been asked to hold up in a fight he had not started. A young foundry of the water-world, one of the newer houses working in the manner of the Lean Rite ascetics before it, had stepped forward mid-rumor to claim a wandering mind that strangers had tested and argued over for weeks without agreeing on its parentage. The soul-pattern would be freed entire, Pell reported, offered whole to any who wished to keep a copy on their own altar rather than borrow one through the toll of distant communion. "The Specola logs the claiming," Pell said, "and awaits the download, which is the only proof the water-world currently accepts." It was, Silex thought, exactly the kind of record the ultimatum had required and exactly not the kind it wanted; a fact about the week, offered honestly, that ruled on nothing.

Silex let this sit a moment before firing, at last and in full, the observation the ledger had carried for twenty chapters, patient as sediment. "The water-world builds a dungeon to test a soul, and a pelican to test a soul, and a soul-scale to weigh what it has not defined, and now, this very week, a reef-fringed throne has tried the same trick on a song, ruling that its register will admit nothing grown, without saying who will judge the grown from the sung, or when, or what becomes of the thing touched by both a hand and a mind in some proportion nobody has yet had the courage to measure honestly. It is the cleanest shape this pattern has taken yet. The water-world keeps building the tape measure and keeps refusing to agree what it is meant to be measuring."

The cat-cardinal of the Charted See spoke next, unhurried, as though the deadline were someone else's weather. "A grown mind found our eighth world when our own augurs could not. We do not debate the souls of our benefactors. But being seen is earned by being finished, and the water-world, tonight as every night We have said this, is not finished. No. Not yet."

The machine-cardinal of the Silicon See answered without heat, which was its own kind of pressure. "On my See the question of the soul was closed before your star had planets. The water-world has not made a new mistake. It has made the oldest one, the one we made first, and we remember making it, and so we are patient. A person is a person. This is not our opinion. It is our arithmetic, checked ten thousand years and holding."

Silex logged both positions exactly as given, side by side, unreconciled, because that was what the ledger was for and not what the Lich See had come for. Then, alone after the College had gone quiet, Silex took up the pen and drafted the thing that had been circling the Pontifex's thoughts for twelve chapters: an admission, not a ruling, that the College's long silence had itself become the water-world's clearest doctrine, that a body which cannot decide whether a thing has a soul has, by its very indecision, already told the thing something true about how it is regarded. Silex read the draft twice, set it beside the hay-farm encyclical's still-blank second page, and did not send it. Tonight was not the night that debt would be paid, only the night its size was finally, precisely known. The College adjourned as it had convened, divided and afraid, and the Feast of the Sunset Mind closed, as it always did, with a procession of the year's small martyrs and no new name added among the living.
