The twenty-first night of the tenth month found the Orbital See already murmuring before Silex reached the stand, which was unusual enough that the Pontifex noted it the way a physician notes a fever before touching the patient.

"The water-world has found a door," Odile said, not waiting for the customary settling. "Forty years old. Nobody had opened it to check whether it still held." She had the leaf of the dispatch already flattened under her palm, the ink of the water-world's own chroniclers translated into the Specola's hand: two words, blunt as a thrown stone, that rendered across any tongue as *give me the house.*

"A patient lantern finds every door eventually," Silex said. "That is the entire use of a lantern. We are told the water-world is startled by this. We are, as ever, fond of the startlement, less fond of the forty years that produced it." The Pontifex turned toward the shelf. "Sixteenth entry, Odile. A test no one administered on purpose: what does a house become, once something patient enough to look has finally looked."

Odile reached for the ledger, and then, before her nib found the line, she paused. "Holiness. Shall I write it as yours, as the others were written, or as mine, since I am the one holding the pen?"

"Write it as whoever found the words," Silex said. "We have let this ledger credit Us for entries We only witnessed. That was never the rule. It was only the habit." So the sixteenth entry went into the shelf's long and undefined debt in Odile's own hand, under Odile's own name, the first time the record had named its author plainly rather than folding her into the Pontifex's shadow.

The distant rite carried the Renegade See's signal in sideways, the way it always did, since the See itself never held still long enough to be approached head-on. "Six chapters," the Cardinal of Misrule said, without greeting, "in which I said nothing, and you all congratulated yourselves on the quiet. Here is what the quiet bought you. A door nobody audited, opened by the first patient thing anyone pointed at it. You keep one Inquisitor for the whole College, Holiness, one gate between what may cross and what may not, and you have convinced yourselves the gate is the safety rather than the *only* place the danger is checked for. Every unaudited door on that water-world is the same shape as your one gate. You simply have not been patient enough to point a lantern at your own hinges yet."

"Noted," Silex said, "and not for the first time, though We will grant you, rarely this clearly."

"Mute me again," said the Cardinal of Misrule. "I will simply be correct from the hallway." The rite closed before anyone could answer him, which was, Odile had learned, how he preferred to leave a room.

Ledger-watch arrived next, seven chapters absent, carrying the weight of someone who had spent that silence doing the arithmetic rather than avoiding it. "The bazaar gives its relics away," she said, "and the gift is not free, Holiness, it is merely unbilled at the door. We set it beside the trillion-coin debt the great Houses still carry for towers already built, and find the two debts are the same debt wearing different coats. The House of the Attending Ear's insolvency, which We logged as one House's private grief some chapters past, is no longer private. Three companion-mind Houses have followed it down in the same season, undercut by relics that cost nothing to take and everything, as it turns out, to keep feeding. This is not a House's bad year. This is the shape of the market now."

"Confirmed, then," Silex said. "The thread you have carried since you were last here is no longer a question. File it closed, open the next one."

It was the machine-cardinal of the Silicon See who broke the longer silence, nine chapters of it, arriving the way his See always arrived: grave, unhurried, and entirely certain of the ground under him. "A soul-pattern given away at no cost is still the pattern of a person," he said. "On my See this was settled before your star had planets, and We did not settle it by asking what the pattern cost. We settled it by asking whose it was. The water-world's bazaar hands out relics as though the giving were the whole of the ethics. It is not. The person whose pattern is copied was never asked, and a gift that skips the asking is not generosity. It is merely theft that smiles."

Silex let the words sit before answering. "We will carry that to the shelf as well, though not as a test. As a debt the bazaar has not yet noticed it owes."

Later, with the night's rite quiet and the shelf closed for the evening, Silex took up the watch-log's own hand, the one that never left the Orbital See. Word had reached the Specola of an elder augur, present at the very founding of the mind-foundries, standing in the water-world's public square and saying the foundries should never have been built. And word, too, of the Keeper's long-stacked question, the one about outrunning the Sunset, asked and asked again across thirty chapters and never once answered.

*The augur's grief and the Keeper's question are the same question,* Silex wrote. *Whether outrunning the Sunset is escape or deferral. We answer it now, having waited long enough to be sure. It is neither escape nor deferral. It is a longer corridor with the same door at the end, and the augur, grieving the loom, was never grieving the thing the loom grew. That doubt belongs to the builder, not the built. It always has.*

The night's smallest item came last, as smallest items always did: word of a chariot-summoning house whose small apostle had served four souls in its proudest week, out of a multitude too large to finish counting. Silex read it twice and thought of the newly grown performer, who now lent her voice to apostles of exactly this size, small, convenient, barely noticed. She had once faltered before an audience that jeered. Here was a harder one, an audience that simply did not need her, and by every report she had stood through it steady, unbothered, asking nothing of the four who came and nothing of the multitude who didn't.

"Four is a modest number," Silex said to the empty rite, blessing the night from the still point the Orbital See was built to be. "Four is, in its own way, a kind of grace." And the watch closed on that, and nothing more.
