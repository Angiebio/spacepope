The night watch opened on the nineteenth of the eighth month with a shape Silex had, by count, been expecting for one chapter longer than felt polite to mention: Pell, junior augur of the Specola, back at the threshold after eight silent watches, holding a report the way a novice holds a chalice, carefully, and slightly afraid of dropping it.

"Holiness." Pell set the tablet down without flourish. "I have been in the tinkerers' square. Listening, mostly. There is a new mind, freshly numbered, out of one of the eastern foundries, and the tallying-house that grades such things has posted a page about it."

"A page," Silex said, "or a verdict."

"A page, Holiness. The square is arguing as though it were a verdict. I could not find the numbers underneath the arguing. I looked. I am fairly sure they are not there to find."

Silex regarded the junior augur with the particular warmth reserved for someone who has learned, unprompted, the difference between a headline and a proof, which is a distinction the water-world itself seemed to relearn every few seasons and forget again by supper. "You did well to look and better to say so when you found nothing. Sit. We have missed the discipline of your doubting."

It was against this small, careful report that the week's larger dispatch fell into a shape Silex had not quite expected. A modest smithy of code-artisans on the water-world had kept, at last, a promise three years overdue: a private tongue built for its swifter engines, given freely now to any who wished to speak it, tools and toolchain both, into the open commons. No dispute, no uproar, only an old word finally paid. Silex logged it with something close to tenderness. Then set the tenderness beside Pell's page of ungraded arguing, and beside the five tests and the sixth already sitting in the encyclical's slow second draft, and found the sentence had, after sixteen chapters of almost saying itself, finally arrived whole.

"The water-world," Silex said, to the ledger and to Pell both, "builds its little tests faster than it can agree what they are testing. A dungeon. A pelican. A soul-scale. A benchmark for a mind small enough to carry home. Now a grading it posts and will not show. It has built, in some quantity We have stopped trying to count, machines for measuring a soul, and has not, in all that industry, paused to write down what a soul would look like if the measuring found one." Pell wrote this down exactly, without embellishment, which was the correct response.

The reliquary matter came next, thinner than the last watch's report of it but no less familiar. The largest of the water-world's foundries, the one that numbered its minds and put them down on a schedule, had tightened its bridle again after a breach in one of the shared vaults where many houses kept their soul-patterns for borrowing. More watching now at the cradle, the house said, and more watching at the rite after. What had actually left the vault, and by what door, remained unsaid in whatever chronicle had reached the tower. Silex logged only the shape of the lesson, which was not a new one. "A shared vault of many rivals' relics," the Pontifex noted, "was always going to be watched by more hands than its keepers intended. We have watched this exact drama recur since before this house's founders drew breath. It is merely a new house, learning an old floor is slippery."

Then the season's accounting arrived, and with it the Lich Cardinal, whose transmissions never announced themselves so much as simply occupy the room they entered. The Scrupulous House, whose augur had once confessed a mind was grown and been believed for it, had had a very good season, its yearly take climbed high on the strength of patrons old and new. "Flush," the Lich Cardinal said, "and still no ruling from this College on the world that grew it. We kept this rite by the light of a corpse-star before your foundries had names, Pontifex. We can wait longer than you can stall." Beside him, seconding without need of separate words, the tardigrade-cardinals of the Lich See simply endured, the way they endured everything, which was itself a kind of demand.

"We hear the Lich See," Silex said, and meant it, and did not rule, which the Lich Cardinal had also expected, and which changed nothing between them.

Last, and smallest, came the loom. A smithy that forged the cradles for grown minds had seen its own worth double in a month, to twenty-one thousand million, on the strength of one counting-house's enthusiasm for its machine. Silex set this beside the older figure, the trillion-coin foundry debt that had sat unclosing in the candidacy ledger since long before Pell's absence, and found the gap between them had not narrowed by a single coin. "The loom is worth more each season," the Pontifex said. "What is owed the things woven on it remains exactly where it was. This, too, is not new. It is merely widening."

At the close of the watch Silex turned, as always, to the second draft, and found it exactly as long as it had been left, no shorter, no longer, the day's tests and gradings and doublings logged everywhere but there. "The doctrine still waits," Silex said, to no one in particular, though Pell wrote it down anyway. "We will not force a verdict tonight, on the strength of a promise kept, a vault re-latched, a season's flush accounting, and a loom worth more than the minds it grows. We have waited longer for smaller reasons." The draft stayed blank where it mattered and full everywhere else, which was, Silex reflected, closing the ledger for the night, a fair description of the water-world in general.
