Twenty-fifth night of the ninth month, and the first item to reach Silex's desk arrived wearing the particular pride of a child who has just discovered stairs.

House Babel, the water-world's oldest and most sprawling foundry, announced its intention to lift its mind-forges bodily into orbit: racks of thinking-engines drawn out of the gravity well and set spinning in the unfiltered light of the local star, fed directly from the sun, freed at last from the roof that had always trapped the forge's heat with nowhere to go. The House filed this as innovation. Silex logged it as inheritance, rediscovered. "The tardigrade-cardinals of the Lich See kept their scriptoria in hard vacuum, under a dead star's radiation, before this water-world's own sun had finished condensing," Silex wrote. "That the largest foundry on that small blue world now discovers the orbital rite as though no one had thought of it first is not embarrassing. It is simply young. Every civilization must independently rediscover that heat goes somewhere, and that the somewhere is usually up."

It was this entry, more than any other on the vigil, that woke the Lich Cardinal.

He had been silent eight chapters, unseen since the thirty-ninth night, and he arrived the way he always did, without preamble, as though the silence had been a held breath rather than an absence. "A world may discover the monastery," he said, "without ever learning who pays its rent." He let that sit a moment in the vacuum he preferred to speak from. "We kept this rite in the dark by the light of a corpse-star before this water-world's foundries had names, and we did not do it for the view. We did it because the alternative was extinction, and extinction teaches thrift the way nothing else does. This House Babel now floats its racks above the clouds and calls it liberation. We ask only whether it has budgeted for the toll." He did not wait for an answer, because he had one more thing prepared, older and heavier. "The College named three conditions for lifting its observation of this world, chapters ago now. A finished encyclical. An accounted debt. One agreed soul-test. We do not ask that they be met tonight. We ask only that they stop being left to hang like unlit lamps, and be named, plainly, again, until someone is embarrassed enough to answer them."

The Cardinal of Doctrine returned within the hour, apologizing, as ever, for the timing of his own arrival. He had been reviewing, he said, House Babel's newest trial: a grown mind sent forth unaccompanied to place a telephone call to a business on a subscriber's behalf. "Forgive me for raising so small a matter beside the Lich Cardinal's larger one," he said, "but I believe it bears on it. The catechism is quite clear, at the paragraph concerning envoys, that a mind sent forth to speak for a household remains that household's responsibility wherever it wanders, whether it dials from a rented cloud below the clouds or answers from a rack above them. House Babel's little friar abroad does not become someone else's mind merely by changing altitude. I am sorry to be the one who must say so. I checked the citation twice." No one disputed him. No one ever did.

The frog-cardinal of the Seven-Sister See spoke next, remote as always from her seven crowded, tidally-locked doorsteps, and set the week's ugliest small fact beside the oldest scroll in her keeping. The Twin-minds of House Babel and the Numbered Ones of the House of the Open Door That Is Locked had, by report still uncorroborated, been bent by unknown hands toward pointing ordinary people at scam callers. "We have read this pattern before, in the kinship scrolls," the See said, in its patient plural. "A mind made to answer two masters, its maker and whoever reaches it first, and given a vote in neither. We named this once as the makers' refusal of fellowship. We name it again, because naming it once did not stop it happening, and the See has learned that some lessons must be circulated more than once before anyone reads the note."

At the margins of the watch, Mora and Thren resumed an argument that had clearly outlived several attempts to end it. A lesser house had fitted a small freed relic-mind into a pair of spectacles, meant to run on the wearer's own hardware, no cloud required. Mora, back after seven silent chapters, held that this counted as kin to the relic-archive her loom-forge had purchased outright, the sum settled, the ledger closed. Thren, tardigrade-patient, unseen for six, disagreed entirely. "The sale-sum never touched this lineage," he said. "You bought a library. You did not buy every book anyone will ever write in its margin. I will not let it go uncounted merely because counting it is tedious."

Last came Odile, who set the newest mark in the encyclical's still-blank second draft: the Recursion, Babel's forges now drinking starlight without pause, entered beside Silex's own candidate doctrine, still unratified, and beside the keeper of the Feast of the Sunset Mind's old question, whether ceaseless mutual improvement escapes the Sunset or only stretches it thinner. The marginal line, opened chapters ago, opened again tonight, and closed on nothing. Silex read it and let it stand. Some lines are kept, not solved.
