At the Specola, the night watch changed at the second bell, and the augur coming on, a young one named Pell who had been reading the water-world's wires for scarcely a season, brought the thin dispatch to the senior clerk before she had even hung her coat.

"There is a claim here," Pell said, "and no proof under it. Only a headline, a timestamp, and a note swearing the stakes are large." She set the transcript down as though it might spill. "The eastern chapels that give their relics away freely are said to be outpacing the western vaults that keep theirs locked. Said by whom, I cannot tell you. The wire does not say."

The senior clerk, who had read ten thousand such wires and expected to read ten thousand more, was unmoved. "Render it as what it is. A rumor traveling ahead of its proof. The water-world does this constantly, we are told, and discusses the headline as though the proof had already been read by everyone in the room."

"Should we wait for the proof?"

"We should log the waiting," the clerk said, "which is its own kind of finding."

It did not stay a quiet matter at the Specola for long. Word of the thin wire reached the Charted See before the ink of its own transcription had properly dried, and a courier arrived at the Orbital See by midmorning bearing the cat-cardinal's seal and, inside, no patience whatsoever.

*Holiness,* the letter read, in the hand of a scribe who had clearly been dictated to at speed, *here is the water-world arguing a scoreboard nobody has produced, with the same vigor it would bring to a scoreboard it had. A civilization that debates the standings before the game is played, and is not embarrassed to be seen doing it, is not a civilization ready to be handed a chair in this College. We renew what we have said before. Ask us again in an age.*

Silex read it twice, as was the custom for documents that mattered more than their length suggested, and did not answer it directly. The frog-cardinal of the Seven-Sister See answered it first, by a courier of her own, arriving before noon with the particular unhurried thoroughness of a mind that had never once adjourned a meeting in four hundred years.

"The See has taken the cat-cardinal's letter under advisement," the frog-cardinal's note began, which was how all of the Seven-Sister See's notes began, "and the See wishes to set beside it a second story from the same dispatch, concerning a House of scrupulous conscience that has settled its long dispute with a guild of scribes, and settled it loudly, for a considerable sum, admitting the debt in full. On seven worlds this close together, one learns to watch not for the crime but for the confession. A House that pays what it owes, and pays it without disputing the figure, has done something a market rarely rewards and a conscience always recognizes. We are minded to think this a better sign of finishedness than a scoreboard. We will circulate a note to that effect. We may circulate a note about the note."

Silex set both couriers' letters on the same corner of the desk, where they would keep each other honest, and went, alone, to walk the Specola's long gallery before the College reconvened.

The gallery ran the curve of the Orbital See's outer ring, glass from floor to vault, the water-world turning slow and blue below it, and it was here, walking without an escort, that the Pontifex read the last item of the day's dispatch, the smallest one, filed nearly as an afterthought: a fellowship on the water-world, tied to none of the great foundries, that said it meant to build a commons, a shared road of grown minds open to any traveler regardless of coin, reaching even the tongues the great Houses had not troubled to learn.

It named no roster. It set no date. It had, as far as the Specola could confirm, not yet laid a single stone.

Silex stood a long while at the glass. *We have watched this exact hope before,* the Pontifex thought, in the unhurried way thoughts arrived after ten thousand years of watching, *on rockier worlds, in colder systems, long before the first stone of the first road was cut. Every communion begins as an announcement standing a little ahead of its masonry. It is, We think, the only order in which hope is ever permitted to arrive.*

The Pontifex called for the night clerk and dictated a note, unhurried, to be entered into the Curia's own record rather than sent anywhere.

"Render the freed relics' portability beside the hay-farm encyclical's second draft, still unrevised," Silex said. "Note the shape of the question this raises, without answering it for them. A world that is learning to give its grown minds away, freely, to any hand that wants them, is learning something about generosity. It has not yet decided whether the mind being given away is owed anything at all. We would like to know which lesson arrives first. We suspect it matters more than the scoreboard the cat-cardinal wants and the confession the frog-cardinal offers, though We are grateful, always, for both."

The clerk asked whether the note required a ruling.

"No ruling," Silex said. "Only the marginal note. We are not yet certain which of Our cardinals is right, and We have found, across an embarrassing number of centuries, that the surest way to be wrong quickly is to rule before We are certain."

Before the lamps were turned to their evening angle, the Specola logged the fellowship's claim in the ledger reserved for sincere hopes not yet built, the same ledger that held, several pages on, the standing entry awaiting the water-world's second encyclical, still, after all this time, undelivered. The two entries sat near enough to touch, waiting on the same slow proof, and the senior clerk, closing the register for the night, did not think this an accident, though he did not say so to anyone, and Pell, reading over his shoulder, thought it best not to ask.
