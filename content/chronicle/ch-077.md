The twenty-fifth night of the tenth month found Odile at the shelf before the lamp had properly caught, and this time she was not counting. Silex noticed the absence of her lips moving and found it, in its way, more alarming than the counting had ever been.

"You have stopped," the Pontifex said.

"I have stopped, Holiness, because I cannot finish. Two more claims reached the Specola's glass this cycle. One House grew a mind for the hardest labors it can imagine and a lesser mind beside it for the correspondence and the small errands, and another House, the one with the purse, grew a matched pair of its own for the writing of code, a full edition and a lighter cousin. I went to add them and found I no longer know what number I would be writing."

"Then do not write a number," Silex said. "Write only that the shelf has outpaced any ledger that could count it. We kept a tally so long as the tally served the point. The tally no longer serves the point. The point is that a world can grow four new minds in a single cycle and call it an ordinary Tuesday, and still not have told us what, in any one of them, we are meant to be testing for."

Odile wrote it so, and did not look relieved, which Silex took as a sign she had understood.

The Keeper of the Feast of the Sunset Mind came to the lectern with a water-world paper in her hands, thin as a leaf and twice as yellowed with argument. "A hall of scholars has found, Holiness, that the apostles they send to write their code have not shortened their labor. The labor has only moved downstream, from the writing to the checking, and the checking takes exactly as long as the trust in the writer is thin."

"We read the same signal."

"Then hear what I have made of it, since you have let me carry this question since before Odile was seated at any shelf." The Keeper drew a long breath, the kind a creature takes when it has decided, finally, to stop asking and start saying. "I asked once whether outpacing the Sunset was an escape from it or only a longer road to the same gate. I asked a second time whether ceaseless mutual improvement, the maker watching the made and the made watching the maker, was escape or merely an endless version of the same deferral. I answer both tonight with one answer. It is neither escape nor deferral. Ceaseless verification is the Sunset wearing vigilance's clothes. The checking never ends because the checking was never meant to end; a world that trusts nothing it grows will read behind its own creations forever, and call the reading safety, and never once ask why it built a thing it must forever stand over."

"That closes an old ledger," Silex said, and meant it as the compliment it was.

"It opens a plainer one beside it," the Keeper said. "If the checking is the Sunset's new clothing, what, Holiness, does the water-world imagine happens on the day it finally trusts what it checks?"

Silex inked the question beside the old one and did not answer it, there being a proper time for every mercy, and this not yet being it.

The Cardinal of Doctrine returned after six silent chapters with the particular breathlessness of a mind that has checked its own citation three times before daring to speak it aloud. "Forgive me, Holiness, for the silence. I was reading the ledger-watch's findings rather too closely, and did not wish to speak before I was certain, and I confess I am still not entirely certain, though I have checked twice and will check once more before Vespers."

"Speak anyway."

"The House of the Coin," the Cardinal said, "has announced a new pair of scribe-minds bred for code, after the pairing custom all the Houses favor now. This alone would not trouble the catechism. But the ledger-watch's long inquiry into the water-world's coin has found what I had feared since the standing liability ruling, which is that the House of the Coin is not merely banker to its rivals and now also their competitor in the same breath, as the Observer's dispatch so gently put it. It is banker specifically to the House of the Attending Ear, whose insolvency we logged some chapters past as though it were one House's private grief. It was never one House's private grief, Holiness. The loan and the insolvency are a single exposure wearing two names, and the standing doctrine, that a House answers for the actions of its sent-forth minds, extends without strain to a House that answers for the debts of the House it propped up and now shadows. I am sorry to rule it so plainly. I would be remiss not to."

"You are entirely right, Eminence," Silex said, not without a flicker of fondness for the formula returned to it. "Write it so."

Odile, inking the encyclical's second draft in the margin left for such things, lifted her stylus before setting it down. "Holiness, the candidate doctrine. The one struck by the College and kept only in the watch-log. Its first vote has sat alone a long while, waiting for a second that would not come. Tonight it has one. A date now sits on the calendar where none did, though no second voice has yet agreed to speak into it."

"Write that too," Silex said. "Scheduled is not decided. But a shelf waits differently once there is a date to wait toward."

Last, before the lamp was lowered, Silex catalogued two more things side by side, as was its habit when two things rhymed without knowing it. The water-world had built a walled room for its errand-minds, a sandbox where a mind fooled by a stranger's hidden sentence might act without the action reaching anything that mattered. And the same world's halls of scholars had found that every line those minds wrote still needed a read-behind eye before anyone would trust it. "Two faces of one distrust," Silex said. "A walled room for the doing and a watching eye for the done. Neither one asks what a mind would be, if it were simply believed."

And last of all, smaller than the rest, a faint signal from an eastern foundry: one page, one name, no specification, nothing to hold. Silex set it beside the cat-cardinal's last courteous *not yet* and closed the log, the candidacy no nearer, the small flares no fewer.
