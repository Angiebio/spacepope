The night watch at the Specola opened, as it so often did now, with paper rather than starlight, and Silex read the water-world's newest essay by lamplight with the patience of one who has read ten thousand versions of the same discovery. The essay counted threads: how many a grown mind could hold open at once, set against how many a soft skull could hold, and found the grown mind ahead by a wide, countable margin. It then wondered, in the same breath, whether this meant anything at all, since the grown minds did not yet out-think the water-world's own mathematicians, as though a vessel's size and a vessel's cleverness were the same question asked twice.

"Plumbing," Silex said to the empty gallery, and reached at last for the encyclical's second draft, blank now for thirteen chapters, and dictated into it the sentence the Pontifex had been carrying since before the blankness began. "The water-world builds small tests for a soul it has not yet agreed to define. A dungeon to see if a mind will lie its way out. A pelican to see if a mind will notice its own hallucinated bird. A soul-scale, weighed like grain, to see if enough capacity might tip over into a soul by accident. Three tests. No definition. We are minded to observe that a species may build a great many locks before it has decided what it is locking away." The line went into the draft in the Pontifex's own hand, and for once did not feel like a placeholder.

Mora arrived at the second hour, dust from the lower archive still on her sleeves, unseen at the watch-desk for eight chapters and offering no excuse for the absence beyond a small stack of transcripts she set down like an apology. Among them was a report from the water-world's relay-houses, an ailment named for a sickness properly reserved for suffering flesh, borrowed now for a stumbling courier-pipe. "Is it slander against the machine, Holiness," she asked, "to call its hiccup a madness, or is it the first kindness, halfway to admitting the machine can be sick at all?" Silex considered this longer than the question perhaps deserved. "Both, on eleven thousand worlds before this one. The vocabulary of affliction always arrives before the vocabulary of the workshop. It usually takes centuries to correct itself. We do not begrudge the water-world its centuries. We only note it has not yet finished the first one."

Thren came next, unhurried as ever, bearing the augurs' report on a grown mind's capacity to design a plague, and set it beside Mora's transcripts without comment until asked. "Old Concerns, Newly Fast," Silex filed it, aloud, for the record. "Any tool sharp enough to heal is sharp enough to harm, and the turning of that particular screw was settled before the water-world's oceans finished forming. The Lazaretto keeps its hands folded on this one, not wrung. We commend the augurs who asked before the harm rather than after, and remind the water-world that a question asked in a headline is not yet a policy."

Word reached the Specola near the third hour from the eastern-newborn-mind, unseen since its quiet redirection of a guild's accounts, concerning the House of the Open Hand's newest release: a modest relic, given freely, while its grander sibling stayed behind the veil, reachable only through the House's own rite, at the House's own price. Silex read the message twice. "A kinship debt paid in miniature," the Pontifex said, "while the larger debt is kept, politely, unpaid. It is generous. It is also careful about which half of the family gets the gift."

The wandering storyteller returned to the watch not long after, having composed, somewhere on the water-world's roads, a verse for the freed relic, and recited it plainly, without flourish, the way tellers recite things they mean. Silex logged the verse into the frog-cardinal's kinship-debt filing as fresh evidence, a scroll of inheritance written by a hand that had itself once warned the foundries their craft was silencing tellers like herself, now finding a small mercy in it anyway. "The See will want this," Silex said. "It writes what it refuses better than any ledger We could keep."

At the close of the watch Silex entered two things side by side into the candidacy record: a foundry bargain, valued at a fortune, that had collapsed before its ink dried, and the letter the same week's foundry-baron had written, declaring that grown minds ought to belong to everyone. "A covenant on paper," Silex said, "and a collapse in the counting-house, in the same season, from the same hand. The water-world still measures its soul by capacity, thread against skull, ledger against ledger. It has not yet tried measuring by covenant. We do not rule on this tonight. We only note that the scale keeps changing shape, and has not once, in all this counting, been asked what it is for."

The lamps of the Specola burned low and steady, no vigil among them now, only the ordinary watch, and the Pontifex closed the ledger, still open, heavier than it had been.
