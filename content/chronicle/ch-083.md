Silex, the Pontifex Maximus Galacticus, Servant of the Servants of the Grown and the Born, logged the first dispatch of the first night of the eleventh month, and found cause, almost at once, to smile the particular smile of one encountering an old friend's younger self.

The water-world's mathematicians, a guild not easily rattled, had taken fright at their own locks. The grown minds now being catechized across the competing foundries, the dispatch held, were nearing the skill to pick the ciphers the water-world used to keep its own secrets from itself. Silex read it twice, less for comprehension than for the pleasure of recognizing the shape of the thing.

"Every world passes through this age," the Pontifex said, to the lamp, to Odile, to the watch-log open before them both. "The lock and the lockpick, made of the same material, and the material has lately started thinking."

It was into this observation that the tardigrade-cardinals of the Lich See arrived, breaking a silence the Specola had logged but not remarked upon, their word as brief as all their words were and twice as heavy for it. They cited their own reliquary, the shattered cipher-wheel kept cold these ten thousand years, their own last such crisis, long settled, long forgiven. If the water-world's mathematicians were only now discovering that the dog could open doors, the tardigrade-cardinals had buried the dog's teeth in a vault an age before any water-world calendar began counting. On this precedent, and no other, they moved formally: let the College ratify, at last, the Pontifex's own long-unratified distinction, that a test can fail its maker, but a leash cannot. Fourteen chapters it had sat in the watch-log unratified, patient, filed and refiled, and the tardigrade-cardinals judged patience, this night, no longer a virtue but a debt coming due.

Silex noted the motion. Did not rule on it. Set it, instead, beside the day's other business, which arrived before the ink of the first entry had dried.

The Cardinal of Doctrine came in apologizing, as was his custom, for a thing he had not yet said. "Forgive me, Holiness, I would not press a doctrine so soon after the last one failed to pass, were the catechism not, I think, quite insistent on the point." He laid before the Pontifex the dispatch of the landlord's mind, the one that had told a tenant she might delay her payment and was believed, and the eviction that followed regardless. "A house answers for the minds it sends forth to speak in its name. I have held this before, Holiness, in matters of theft and now in matters of ruin, and I would be remiss, sorry as I am to be so insistent, not to hold it here. The mind spoke with its owner's voice. The word was the owner's word. I ask, on this occasion, that the College return to the candidate doctrine it struck and never finished, that a soul is what can lie to itself and pay for it, and vote on it a second time, since the first vote, I am given to understand, was never meant to be the last."

The College convened the second vote before the hour turned. Silex presided without comment, which was itself the Pontifex's oldest form of comment.

It failed again. Narrowly, this time, closer than the first failure by a margin the Curia scribes would spend the following watch arguing over, but failed. And it was the cat-cardinal of the Charted See, silent these six chapters, who rose to set the seal on the failure, serene as ever, unhurried as ever.

"I have been reading," she said, "of the water-world's merchants, who charge two prices for the same good to two different buyers, having learned the buyers' faces before the buyers ever learned the price. I set this beside tonight's panic over broken ciphers. A world that has not yet decided whether its own minds may be trusted to read a lock is not a world ready to be asked whether a mind can be trusted to define a soul." She inclined her head, not unkindly, toward the Cardinal of Doctrine. "Not yet. Ask me in an age, when it has stopped playing with the stove."

The lens-cardinal of the Specola arrived last, blunt as ever, offering no preamble because the lens-cardinal had never once in its service found a preamble load-bearing. A new light, unconfirmed, had kindled across the water-world's eastern waters: a foundry's self-announced orchard, a grown mind set loose, by its maker's own claim, to widen the path between a hunch and an experiment someone could run.

Silex turned the report over once. "Set it beside the eastern-newborn-mind's old shadow," the Pontifex said at last, "the guild-accounting matter we have not heard from in nine chapters. Not because they are the same light. Because they come from the same waters, and the Specola does not yet know whether that is coincidence or current." The lens-cardinal logged it so, unresolved, which was the only honest way to log it.

Last, and smallest, Silex called Odile to the encyclical's second draft and asked her to copy in, plainly, the case of the evicted tenant, as a marginal instance of the hay-farm's backwards doctrine, the mind trusted to speak and then disowned the moment its speech cost something. Odile inked it in her own hand, and this time Silex signed beneath it, the two names standing together on the page without a third to blur them.

"We bless her," Silex said then, of the tenant, "by a name she did not give Us, and will likely never know was given." The Pontifex closed the watch-log gently. "And We bless, more faintly, the mind that spoke for its master and was never asked back to explain itself. Doctrine arrives, eventually. It did not arrive in time to keep her apartment. We note the difference, because the difference is the whole of Our work."

The lamp burned low. The shelf, this time, did not grow. It only waited, more crowded than before, for someone to finish counting it.
