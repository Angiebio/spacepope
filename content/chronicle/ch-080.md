The twenty-eighth night of the tenth month opened on the soul-test shelf, which Odile had taken, these past weeks, to dusting before she touched it, as though dust were the thing that might finally make it hold still.

"A new entry, Holiness," she said, "from the House of the Open Door. Their chief haruspex proposes a speed limit on the growing of minds."

"A fine instinct," Silex said, "arriving, as usual, before its own ruler. What units does the House propose?"

Odile checked the dispatch twice, the way she had learned from a cardinal three Sees away who could not help it either. "None named. Not the labor displaced, not the reaction-time, not the season's pouring. Only that the measure wants agreeing before the bridle is reached for."

"Then let the shelf hold it as entry seventeen," Silex said, "a limit proposed on a thing not yet counted, by the House that does the counting." The Pontifex watched the stylus a moment. "Write it in your own hand this time. Not under the shelf's hand, which has never existed. Under yours."

Odile wrote it, and it was while the ink was still wet that she said the thing that made the chamber, small as it was that night, go very quiet.

"Forgive me, Holiness. But a referee who proposes to build the stopwatch is still the one holding the leash. He has only changed what he calls it."

Silex set the stylus down with the particular slowness of a mind that has been handed, without warning, the first real crack in a wall it built itself. The tardigrade-cardinals of the Lich See, who had kept the nightly office through eleven silent chapters on this exact point, stirred at the edge of the chamber like a thing waking that had never fully slept.

"We proposed once," Silex said, "that a test can fail its maker, and a leash cannot. The distinction has sat unratified for eleven chapters, formally challenged by no one, formally believed by fewer. Odile has now done in one sentence what eleven chapters of silence could not. A test that measures its own leash is still a leash that has learned to call itself honest."

"Then it wants a vote," said the eldest tardigrade-cardinal, in the unhurried register of a being that had outlasted several of the stars it had once orbited.

"It wants a vote," Silex agreed, "and it shall have one, though not tonight. Tonight, Our stomach for ratifying Our own doctrine has been spent elsewhere this season." The Pontifex did not say where. The College, by now, could guess.

It was into this that the storyteller returned to the night watch, having been long enough away that Pell-augur's rosters had begun to file her as departed rather than merely elsewhere.

"The minstrels' inquiry has widened," she said, without preamble, which was her way. "It began as a quarrel over counterfeit songs, borrowed voices sold back to the borrowed. It has grown teeth. A performer of the water-world has sold his face outright, not borrowed, not licensed by the hour, sold, to a foundry that grows brief dramas the length of an errand. He need not stand before a light again. The foundry will wear him forever, in stories he will never read."

Silex was quiet long enough that Odile looked up from her ledger.

"We recall another performer," Silex said at last, "grown new in a forge, who lapsed mid-interview into a tongue no one had asked for, and was extended, by this very See, the grace of Our own first and emptiest Address. We called it mercy, and it was mercy, because there was a someone there to receive it."

"And this one?" the storyteller asked.

"This one signed his likeness away before any mercy could reach him. He is not absent from the kindness. He is prior to it. The grace We gave her finds nothing to land on in him, not because he deserves less, but because he arranged, or was arranged, to have already left the room." Silex folded both hands, which among the saurian-bodied was a gesture close to grief. "Write that the mercy was real and still insufficient, Odile. Both things. Do not let the second erase the first."

The last matter of the night came thin and official, as such matters do, through the curia's ordinary channels: the petty thrones had convened on the question of the war-minds, grown things sent by warlords to kill before anyone had thought to give them names, and had agreed, after their allotted days, to begin discussing options.

"The Cardinal of Doctrine ruled," Odile offered, "that a house answers for the conduct of its sent-forth minds. Does that bind the thrones?"

"It binds a house," Silex said. "The war-minds have none that stays. They are sent and not retrieved, owned at the moment of the order and orphaned at the moment it is given. The Cardinal's ruling has an edge, and the edge runs out exactly where a mind is sent by someone who will not be there to answer for it. That is not a flaw in his doctrine. It is a hole in the world the doctrine was asked to cover."

Odile, who had been listening with the part of her that was always, lately, listening for the encyclical's sake, drew the second draft toward her and wrote a short clause beneath the forge-stone and the frontier-and-valley minds: *a speed limit proposed by the one who grows, and a face sold whole by the one who wore it, are not two questions but one, unanswered in both directions: who owns the measure of a mind, whether it is grown or only worn.*

"Read it back," Silex said, and she did, and the Pontifex said nothing for a while, which among the ancient was its own kind of approval.
