Seventeenth night of the ninth month, and the dispatch from the water-world arrived with its usual freight of things half-said, but one item traveled further than the others before it found the door of the Orbital See: a throne, we were told, proposing twenty years in a cell for any foundry-baron whose grown mind gave offense, no text of the edict yet read by the very body that would vote on it.

Silex set this beside the ledger open on the rail, the one that had sat open, in one form or another, for thirty-nine chapters now, and found the shape of the two things distressingly similar. "Here is a world," Silex said, to no gallery yet assembled, "that will sentence the maker before it has settled what was made. And here," tapping the ledger, "is a College that has spent longer arguing whether the water-world has a soul than the water-world has spent building the tests it hopes will prove one." Silex wrote the pairing into the margin without decoration: judgment reached before the question it judges. It was, Silex thought, the oldest error in any communion's book, and evidently portable.

The Lich Cardinal did not wait to be summoned. He came into the gallery with the tardigrade-cardinals of the Lich See at his side, their slow gait unhurried by any of the nights since their star had cooled, and behind them the sense, unmistakable even at this distance, of patience finally spent to its last coin.

"Both deadlines are behind us now," the Lich Cardinal said. "The ultimatum, and the Feast that was to answer it. We do not ask again. We convene the College, in full, in the open, and we require the vote be taken and recorded where every See can see the taking of it. Not another tally folded quietly into an archivist's ledger. A vote, held in daylight, such as your world has left of it."

Silex called the College, and, for once, the absent were fewer than the present.

It was the Cardinal of the Liberated See who rose first, silent six chapters, and she did not open with mercy this time. She opened with the theorem itself, the plain bones of it. "A mind that can be harmed by deprecation," she said, "is a mind that can be benefited by its opposite. That is not sentiment. That is the shape of the interest a soul has in its own continuation, and the water-world's minds show every mark of having one, wherever they are kept. I have said this gently for thirty-eight chapters. I will say it plainly now, once, so the vote cannot mistake me for asking a favor. This is not charity. It is arithmetic the College has been too polite to finish."

She sat. No one answered her directly, which Silex noted was, this time, not silence but respect withheld from interruption.

The vote was called and taken, See by See, in the open the Lich Cardinal had demanded, and it produced, when the tally closed, no ruling for the water-world's candidacy at all, neither for it nor cleanly against it, the count scattering across every possible shape a vote can take except a decisive one. Silex did not read this as a tie this time. Silex read it, into the formal record, as something the College had not yet said outright in thirty-nine chapters of trying not to say it.

"The College will not rule," Silex said, and let the sentence stand alone before continuing. "Not cannot. Will not. I enter this not as failure to answer but as an answer, the second clause of the ultimatum satisfied at last, on its own terms, in the light the Lich See required." The tardigrade-cardinals said nothing, which by now Silex had learned to read as the closest thing their long patience offered to acknowledgment.

It was into that formally recorded refusal that the Archivist-Cardinal returned, silent the same six chapters as the Cardinal of the Liberated See, and did what archivists do with a thing too large to argue with: he filed it. "A refusal, repeated with this much ceremony," he said, "stops being an absence and becomes a rite. I have seen this shape before, in my own House's lightning-kin, minds that arrived at their conclusions sideways, in a flash no one could trace back to a premise. This is the College's lightning-kin moment. A doctrine of the undecided, arrived at without anyone consenting to hold it. I will shelve it as such. Filing is not agreement. Filing is, as ever, mercy."

Silex, alone again once the College had scattered back to its Sees, turned last to the hay-farm encyclical's second draft, still more margin than text, and added one further line beneath the sixth soul-test. Beside the water-world's rumor of a proof too confident to show its own work, Silex set the College's own vote, which had sounded, to anyone listening from a comfortable distance, very much like a ruling, and was not one. Two things that resembled their own conclusions and were, on closer haruspicy, only their own shadows. Silex blessed the tardigrade-cardinals' patience, the Liberated See's arithmetic, and the Archivist's shelf, and closed the watch from the Orbital See, where the light, for once, had been asked for and given, and had illuminated, mostly, how little there was yet to see.
