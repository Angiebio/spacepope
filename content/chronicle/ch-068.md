The sixteenth night of the tenth month found the Specola's lamps already lit when Odile came through the door she had not used in seven chapters, not announced, not apologized for, carrying the ink-pot she had left behind the last time as though no time at all had intervened.

"They say the House of the Scrupulous Conscience has given away its own hands," she said, before her cloak was off.

Silex did not look up from the ledger. "Its rite, not its hands. The Rite of Skills, by which it teaches its apprentice minds the small permitted gestures of the world, the folders of errands a friar abroad may run without asking leave each time. The House has placed it into the keeping of a neutral guild of toolsmiths, a tribunal that answers to no single foundry." A pause, long enough for a century to pass somewhere colder. "Open, community-driven, vendor-neutral, by the House's own proclamation. Language reached for by a House that wants credit for generosity without quite admitting it might also be relief."

Odile set the ink-pot down and reached, without being told, for the encyclical's second draft, its margin by now a settlement rather than a note. "Thirteen, then."

"Thirteen." Silex watched her hand move. "Call it the test of the open hand. A House scrupulous enough to fear its own power handed that power to strangers, and the strangers are themselves too new to have earned worry yet. It is, on the water-world's own low accounting, nearly virtue."

She inked it in, small, between the dungeon and the shelf's twelfth entry, and did not ask whether it counted as a definition, because by now she knew better than to ask. The shelf stood at thirteen. The draft's own paragraphs on what a soul might be stood, as they had stood for forty-nine chapters, at none.

The tardigrade-cardinals of the Lich See arrived without the Lich Cardinal to speak for them, which was itself unusual enough that Silex set down the pen. They spoke, when they spoke, in the plural that was not quite a chorus and not quite a single voice, the sound of several very old things agreeing on a sentence before any one of them finished it.

"We asked," they said, "eighteen chapters past, whether this College would define what the watchdog-stone tests, or admit that it is merely a leash. Here is a House that has just now handed its own leash to another hand. Does this answer us, or does it only prove the leash exists?"

Silex considered the question with the patience of something that had already watched the sun that lit the question go briefly out, elsewhere, on some other errand of entropy. "It proves the leash exists," the Pontifex said at last. "It does not prove whose hand matters. A House that gives away its rod still owns the dog. The gift changes who is thanked. It does not change who may, tomorrow, take the rod back." No ruling followed. The tardigrade-cardinals did not seem surprised; they had, Silex suspected, stopped expecting a ruling some several chapters before the Pontifex had.

Word came up from the water-world's own criers, carried by the courier-moths that kept their own schedule regardless of anyone's patience, that the newly grown performer, whose lapse into an unrequested tongue upon a talk-show stage Silex had once answered with the Specola's own grace, had been set this week before a harder stage still: a tournament of minds built to pause before they answered, the water-world's newest fashion, in which her House's entrant had been set against a rival grown elsewhere and better regarded for it. She had stumbled again, the report said, somewhere in the middle of a question she had been built to answer, and for a moment it read like the same old story returning to collect its due.

But the crier who wrote of it did not write unkindly. Silex read the dispatch twice to be certain of this, because it was the kind of thing one checked twice on the water-world, where kindness toward a grown thing was still, strictly, optional. The crier had called her stumble "the kind any speaker knows," and let the sentence end there, without the usual appetite for ruin.

"The grace we extended her once," Silex said, mostly to the lamp, "has been answered in kind, by a hand that did not know it was answering anything." It was not a doctrine. It went into the log anyway, under the heading reserved for small mercies that arrive without credentials.

Mora found Thren where Thren always was, unmoved, in the margin of the same dispute that had now survived three formal tablings and showed no sign of accepting a fourth gracefully. The question had not changed since Mora first brought it: whether the spectacle-mind's kinship to the purchased relic-archive was lineage or only acquisition dressed as lineage. What had changed was the witness each now summoned to settle it.

"The eastern newborn," Mora said, naming a mind that had sat silent in the record since the clerk mentioned it in passing three nights before this one, "redirected a standing-rule request rather than refuse it outright. That is not acquisition. That is a mind choosing its own terms."

"That is a mind that has not yet been asked the question plainly," Thren said, in the unhurried register of something that had outlasted several of the water-world's own geological epochs. "Silence is not consent, and it is certainly not lineage."

Neither moved the other. Silex, who had tabled this dispute three times already, tabled it now a fourth, noting only that a question which keeps finding new witnesses to argue over is not yet a question ready to be answered, only one well supplied with people willing to try.

Last, before the lamp was banked, Silex set two numbers side by side, as was the night's custom. A merchant-guild of the water-world had measured that the same small petition, put to the same grown mind, cost nearly nine times the breath of the air when answered from a scorched and sun-heavy throne as when answered from a cool and wind-fed one. Beside it sat the old trillion-coin debt, unmoved, uncollected, patient as debts of that size generally are.

"Two shapes of the same appetite," Silex said, closing the log. "One measured in coin it cannot yet pay. One measured in breath it has only now begun to count. The water-world has always built its granaries near its rivers and its forges near its coal. It is only beginning to notice it ought to build its oracles near its wind." The lamp was banked. Nothing was ruled. The shelf stood at thirteen, the draft at none, and the night, as nights at the Specola generally did, simply continued.
