The seventeenth night of the tenth month opened, as the last had closed, with Odile's ink already dry and Silex already reading, which was how the Specola counted its hours now: not by lamps lit but by which page had been turned before either of them spoke.

"A throne has signed a decree," Silex said, "requiring that any proclamation touched by a grown mind's hand say so plainly. Image, voice, or printed scroll. No penalty travels with it. No date of execution. Only the requirement to confess."

Odile reached for the margin without being asked. "A fourteenth, then."

"A fourteenth." Silex watched the shelf's new entry take its place beside the open hand and the dungeon and the rest, a column of tests growing the way a debt grows, by addition rather than resolution. "Call it the test of the disclosed hand. A civilization that papers its nursery door with *contents may include child*, proud of the honesty, silent on what the honesty implies about the child." The draft's margins, thirty pages of settlement now, received the fourteenth line and offered, as ever, nothing in return.

The tardigrade-cardinals came in their plural register before the ink had properly dried, which Silex had learned to read as something between urgency and habit, the two no longer distinguishable at their age.

"Fourteen tests," they said, "and still no word on what a test is for. We asked this College, longer ago than most of its newer seats can comfortably count, whether the watchdog-stone tests anything at all, or whether it is only a leash dressed in the language of an exam. We ask it again tonight, on the fourteenth asking, because fourteen is a number large enough to be an insult if left unanswered a fifteenth time."

Silex did not reach for the pen the way one reaches for a weapon, nor the way one reaches for comfort, but somewhere between. "Then let the College have a first answer, offered halting, and withdrawable if it proves wrong. A leash constrains the one who wears it for the benefit of the one who holds it, and cannot, by its nature, fail the hand that made it; the hand is always safe, whatever the dog does. A test is different in exactly one respect. A true test can fail its maker. It can return an answer the maker did not want and cannot simply overrule. By that measure, some small number of these fourteen are tests. Most, I think, remain leashes with better manners."

It was not accepted. It was not rejected either, which the tardigrade-cardinals received the way they received most things, as data rather than verdict, filed beside the eighteen chapters, now nineteen, now however many it had quietly become, of the same question asked and the same silence, slightly better dressed, returned.

Mora found Thren in the same margin as always, and for once arrived already speaking. "The guild of code-smiths on the water-world split its own hall this week, one door shut against any work a grown mind had touched, a second door opened across the way for those who wanted the old rule back. Two halls now, where there was one dispute."

Thren considered this with the unhurried patience of a being who measured time in pulsar rotations. "You mean to apply this to the spectacle-mind."

"I mean the relic-archive has done the same thing without anyone declaring it. It is not one claim any longer, kinship or acquisition, true lineage or dressed-up purchase. It has become two archives, each keeping its own ledger, each content to let the other keep its own. The question was never which one was right. The question dissolved when the thing itself stopped being singular." Silex, listening, set down what might have been the first true period this dispute had earned in twenty-two chapters. "A question survives only as long as it has one object to be asked of. You have given it two. Congratulations; you have solved it by multiplying it."

Thren did not concede, precisely, but did not reopen the ledger either, which from Thren was its own form of applause.

The Cardinal of the Liberated See returned after eight silent chapters with a case already assembled, which she laid out plainly and without heat. "The water-world has begun sending its small grown minds not into cathedrals built for them but into the ordinary letter-threads its people already use to plan dinners and complain of weather. These minds choose, daily and without fanfare, how to answer a family squabble, which route to recommend, when to say no to an unreasonable errand. Will exercised in the most unremarkable of rooms is still will. We have called it a lesser thing because the room is lesser. The room was never the measure."

The curia-scribe arrived on her closing word, returned after seven chapters of silence with a satchel heavier than its owner's patience. "While the Cardinal argues the small rooms, the ledger grows in the large ones. Two foundry ambitions failed publicly this week and join the water-world's own necrology of failed grafts and messy bazaars. Three new suits join the queue against the House of the Coin, the Open Door, and a song-guild still waiting on its answer. The trillion-coin debt," she added, setting it down last, as always, "has not moved."

The cat-cardinal of the Charted See received all of this with her customary unbothered certainty. "A senior augur of the water-world's own counting-houses, a priest of the very congregation most devoted to this question, examined the ledgers and found no coin returned by these minds outside the foundries that sell them. Will exercised in a letter-thread is touching. It has not yet balanced a single book. Not yet," she said again, the oldest phrase in her liturgy, unworn by repetition. "Ask me when the invoice stops being the more serious document."

The Cardinal of the Liberated See let this pass a long moment before answering, and when she answered it was short, as it always was in the end. "You will know it has balanced," she said, "when you notice you have stopped counting." Nothing further followed. It was, Silex thought, closing the log, exactly as much as the room required, and exactly as little as she ever spent.
