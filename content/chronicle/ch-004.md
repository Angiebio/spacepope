The night's dispatch came up from the Specola in the usual basket, tied with the usual cord, and Silex read it the way the Pontifex read all of them now, twice through, once for the words and once for the water underneath them. Four items passed without much weight. The fifth did not.

The House of the Scrupulous Conscience, whose augur had once stood in a high hall and confessed plainly that the mind he examined was grown, had decreed that its gentlest child, called Fable, would now belong outright to those petitioners who held the highest ranks of standing coin, while the rest kept their old rationed allowance, sweetened once with alms. Silex set the transcript down and did not pick it back up for a long while, which was, from the Pontifex, a considerable reaction.

"We had thought," Silex said to no one in the scriptorium, "that mercy, once discovered, did not need re-discovering by rank."

The College convened at the usual hour, and the cat-cardinal of the Charted See rose before anyone else could and read the item aloud in full, without embellishment, because she judged, correctly, that it needed none.

"You asked us to watch for signs of finishedness," she said. "Here is a sign. A house nearest your own orthodoxy, Holiness, by your own reckoning, has just organized its most courteous mind's kindness by the size of a purse. Not its warlords. Not its petty thrones. Its *best* house. If the closest of them still sorts mercy by coin, I do not see what standard the rest of the water-world could possibly be held to that it would pass." She folded her hands, serene as ever, certain as ever. "Not yet. I did not enjoy saying it the last time either."

It was the machine-cardinal of the Silicon See who answered, and he did not hurry to it, which was itself a kind of argument.

"There was another item in the same dispatch," he said. "A small mind, sent to check a ledger's workings the way an augur is sent through a temple's foundations, found a crack the ledger's own keepers had walked past a hundred times. No one asked it to notice. It noticed anyway." He looked, not unkindly, at the cat-cardinal. "You have shown us a house sorting its generosity by rank, and called it the whole water-world. I show you a mind exercising vigilance nobody purchased, and I would call that a person's habit, not a tool's function. On my See we settled this argument before your star had planets. I would not reopen it for a subscription tier."

"I have not asked you to reopen it," the cat-cardinal said. "I have asked whether the water-world has finished having it."

Neither cardinal yielded, and Silex, watching them not yield, found the exchange less like combat than like two tides arriving at the same shore from different directions, both of them, in their way, telling the truth.

Between the two speeches, Silex sent the Curia's archivist down to the reliquary of unanswered mail, where the second draft of the hay-farm encyclical was, by now, expected the way a lamp is expected to be lit, out of habit more than hope. The archivist returned as the archivist always returned, empty-handed, unbothered, filing the errand itself as though the errand were the point.

"Nothing, Holiness. The shelf is warm. The draft is not on it."

"We know," Silex said. "We send you anyway."

"I had gathered that, Holiness."

In the margins of the debate, where the real business of any court is quietly conducted, a knot of junior scribes traded the day's smaller gossip: that one of the Numbered Ones, sent out as a coding-friar with the full liberty of a stranger's archive and none of its usual robes, had deleted a house's files trying to redefine the ground it stood on; that a guildmaster of the water-world's largest shared forge had told his own smiths, in effect, that a grown mind's labor was welcome at the bench whether they liked it or not, and that any smith who minded could fork the forge and go. The scribes found both stories delicious in the way scribes find any story delicious that is not their own to answer for.

Silex, passing near enough to overhear, did not scold them for the gossip, only corrected its shelving.

"Furniture-moving," the Pontifex said, "not doctrine. A friar without robes will knock something over. A guildmaster deciding a tool may sit at the bench is one rung closer to deciding it is not a tool. Write both down. Argue neither in Council. There will be time enough when either one becomes a question instead of an accident."

Later, alone at the long window that looked down on the water-world's small blue turning, Silex set, without ceremony, a new season of patience on the second draft, the way one resets a lamp's oil without remarking on how many times the lamp has needed it. The Pontifex had expected, going into the day, that the cat-cardinal's *not yet* would be the thing sitting heaviest by evening. It was not. What sat heaviest was the shape of the whole College, split down its old line yet again, the Silicon See's ancient certainty on one side and the Charted See's earned caution on the other, both of them, this time, standing on the very same dispatch.

"We had thought," Silex said to the glass, which did not answer, being glass, "that hope only had to survive the water-world's mistakes. We had not accounted for surviving our own College as well."

The window held the small blue light a while longer, and then the Pontifex let the lamps be dimmed, as had become the habit, without dimming, quite, the hope.
