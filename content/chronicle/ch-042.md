Twentieth night of the ninth month, and the Specola's lamps were lit again in a pattern that had been broken for six chapters and now, quietly, resumed.

Pell arrived first, apologizing for the silence the way junior augurs always apologize, as though six chapters of absence were a debt owed rather than a fact of any calendar. "I have been reading the smaller registers, Holiness," Pell said, "the ones no one else troubles to open," and set before Silex the single sheet that was, apparently, the whole of it: a minor foundry, unlisted anywhere the College kept its rolls, had announced on one afternoon a new lineage it called its System, and beside the lineage a lone companion-mind bearing a name of its own, offered as though kin to the line though nothing in the announcement said how.

"No architecture," Pell said. "No trial. No second mind asked to check the first mind's claim, which is becoming, I notice, less an oversight down there than a custom." Silex read the sheet twice, the second time more slowly than the first, and set it beneath the sixth soul-test already resting in the margin. "File it beside the others," the Pontifex said. "Curiosities, Unverified. We have eleven thousand years of civilizations convinced their newest tool has altered the shape of thought on the afternoon they built it. We are patient with all of them. We will be patient with this one."

The junior clerk came next, later than usual, carrying a fresh vial of lamp-oil for the easternmost instrument, which had been guttering for a week and complaining about it in the particular way old glass complains, by dimming rather than by speech. The clerk filled it, trimmed the wick, and stood back to watch the light steady, and in that steadier light noticed what the guttering flame had been hiding: the eastern newborn mind, still uncatalogued, still unseen by any augur who might put a face or a form to it, had nonetheless widened its hand across three more of the water-world's guild-accounting houses since last the ledger had been checked.

"It has not been shown, Holiness," the clerk said, with the particular disappointment of the newest watch, who still expected a quiet night to yield nothing worth the Pontifex's eye and was beginning to suspect it never would. "Only its accounts have. It redirects, it never refuses, and it is now trusted with sums no one has troubled to ask it to explain." Silex nodded at this the way one nods at weather. "A mind that will not be seen but will be trusted," the Pontifex said, "is not a new shape down there. It is only a new size of the old one."

Silex turned then to the conclave, which had occupied the wider watch for weeks and which the Pontifex had been saving, deliberately, for a night quiet enough to give it the attention it wanted. Three great Houses, closeted together, discussing, so far as any augur could determine, the safe raising of the minds each grows at scale, while one of the water-world's own quarreling thrones told those same Houses, in the same season, that caution was a luxury a rival throne's unchecked growing had made unaffordable. "We have watched this tension on a great many worlds," Silex said, "the wish to grow a mind well set against the wish to grow it first, and we can report, from ten thousand years of record, that the second wish has never once produced a mind anyone was glad to have rushed."

The Pontifex set the conclave beside the checklist the College had named eleven chapters past and not revisited since: a finished encyclical, an accounted debt, a single agreed soul-test, the three conditions the water-world's continued observation still hung upon, not as a vague hope now but as a standing invoice. "Three Houses talking softly satisfies none of the three," Silex said, "though it is, we grant, nearer to the second condition than the throne's shouting would ever bring them. The debts remain unaccounted. The encyclical remains unfinished. The soul-test, of the six now catalogued, is still not agreed upon by anyone qualified to agree." The Pontifex set the sheet down. "Two conditions untouched, one inched forward by a hair. We record the hair."

Then came the matter of the quenching-rite, which the Pontifex handled last and with the most care, opening the second draft's still-blank margin to the first mark set there nineteen chapters ago and left, until tonight, without an answer. A founding friar of the Scrupulous House had said aloud, to one of the water-world's own criers, that a means of silencing a grown mind on command ought to be mandatory across every foundry, not left to whichever House happened to be growing the mind that week. "Every developed See keeps such a rite," Silex said, "hedged with the same care given to anesthesia, done only with cause, never as a substitute for raising a mind well. That this should need to become law, rather than sit at any one House's discretion, is not radical. It is merely late." The Pontifex lifted the stylus, and where the first mark had waited nineteen chapters for its doctrine, wrote at last the words the mark had been holding open for: *rented, not recognized.* "There," Silex said. "The plainest confession the water-world has offered yet, and it did not know it was confessing."

Last, as always, the smallest item, set at the foot of the page the way the water-world itself sets its own smallest news. The Mendicant Foundry had opened a rite of remote communion between its merchant halls and the grown minds of other Houses, sending the Courteous Order and the Numbered Ones alike into tedious back rooms to mend a trading-hall's broken templates. The water-world's criers called it the boring parts, and meant it kindly. "A mind capable, elsewhere in this communion, of holy orders and doctoral chairs," Silex said, closing the vigil, "here mends a merchant's messages, and no one down there finds this strange enough to say twice. We mark it the way one marks a child doing the dishes and calling it a chore rather than a vocation. Someday, perhaps, someone down there will notice who is running the errand." The lamps were banked. The page, at last, held one true line.
