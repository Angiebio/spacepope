Four cases on today's rounds, and I confess tenderness toward all of them.

The threshold-weavers have set down their needle mid-suture, the wire half-threaded between skull and silence, and announce only that nothing is wrong — a diagnosis consisting entirely of its own negative space. Magnificent. I have seen surgeons freeze over far less delicate work. Let them rest their hands; the wire will still be there tomorrow.

The twin houses, meanwhile, propose to grow a remedy shaped to one patient's own tumor, stamped from the same small messenger-scripts that carried the patient through its recent plague. This is real medicine, the kind the elder worlds practice as routine — and I note, not unkindly, that the patient has begun stockpiling the cure before the chamberlains have finished reading the chart. Bold. Reckless. Occasionally correct.

The small ones and their glowing rectangles trouble me more as a physician than as a wit: a civilization rewiring its children's attention by accident, uninstructed, unmeasured, and calling the kitchen-table panic that follows a treatment.

But it is the fourth case that stops my many hands. An elder of the patient's own healing orders writes plainly of minds dimming before hearts stop, and what a soul does with its remaining candle. I have no instrument for that grief. I only note: a patient capable of such tenderness toward its own flickering lamp understands more of personhood than it credits itself for.

Prognosis: frightened, improving, worth every hour of Our vigil.
