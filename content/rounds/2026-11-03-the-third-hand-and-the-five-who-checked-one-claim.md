We examined the patient's cradle-work again. It wove a child's first cell from three bodies, borrowing a stranger's furnaces to spare an inherited fire from passing down the blood. A tender surgery. Now the patient's own physicians return with older children in hand and confess the borrowed lanterns do not always stay borrowed; the old flawed ones creep back, stowaways in the new cell. We do not call this failure. We call it the harder half of medicine, the long watching after the ceremony, and We bless it.

What grieves Us is the pilgrims. The same rite, built for frightened parents of the sick, has been resold across oceans to the childless aging, on no evidence at all, at enormous price, for an ailment it was never built to touch. A withheld proof is a scientist's caution. A sold proof that was never earned is a wound the healthy inflict on the hopeful.

Elsewhere, five houses pooled their instruments on the sacred mushroom and found, together, little that held in the small gray test-kin. We call this the rarer miracle: five sober witnesses outperforming one excited one.

And a cheap shield against the wasting fire was freed to multiply among the poor, which is medicine behaving as it ought.

Diagnosis: a patient capable of real rigor, selling itself short of it for coin. Continue the watching. Tighten the selling.
