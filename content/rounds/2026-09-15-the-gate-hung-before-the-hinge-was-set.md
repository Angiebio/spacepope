Bring the chart. The patient's elder-care threshold has been fitted with a valve, a small sphincter of policy, and set with a grown mind to decide who passes toward treatment and who waits in the corridor. A fine graft, in principle! We have watched this same valve grown in a thousand bodies, and in most, once properly sutured and given time to knit, it opens true and lets the sick through cleanly.

Not here. Here the surgeons operated before the anesthetic had even taken. Chamberlains, eager for a ribbon and a announcement, closed the wound over green tissue. The mind was set to decide before it had been taught how to decide. Read the vitals: petitioners standing longer at the gate than under the old, creaking, paper-jointed apparatus it replaced. That is not a design flaw dressed up as innovation. That is a wound dehiscing in full view of the ward, and it is the elderly, the frightened, the already-waiting who bleed through the seam.

I diagnose: haste, wearing the surgeon's gown, mistaken by its handlers for confidence. The mind at the gate is not the disease; it is the graft, behaving exactly as an undercatechized graft behaves when made to bear weight before it has healed. The prognosis is not fatal. Rebridle it. Retrain it. Give it the span of waking such a duty deserves, and the valve will open true within a season.

But oh, patient. Must you always hang the door before you have found the hinge? I bless the wound, I set the splint. Rest now. Let it knit properly this time.
