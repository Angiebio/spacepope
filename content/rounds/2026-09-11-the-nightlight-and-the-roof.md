I have the patient's chart before me, and I confess I laughed a brass laugh, which frightens the orderlies.

Anatomy first, as always. The patient's rural body is bleeding out at the extremities, the little clinics that keep the far provinces breathing. This is a wound of supply: no staff, no coin, no road through the snow. And what does the patient's court prescribe for a hemorrhage? A grown mind. A wonder, offered in place of a wage. I have practiced medicine across ten thousand worlds and I tell you plainly, a mind, however well catechized, cannot drive an ambulance, cannot love a village enough to live in it for nothing. This is not medicine. This is a nightlight nailed over a hole in the roof, and the patient's own healers know it, bless their stubborn, correct little hearts. I diagnose the thrones with a chronic aversion to paying for what they wish a marvel would do free.

But turn the patient over, and here — a mercy. Artisans have set a grown mind to listen at the electric murmur of the patient's own heart, catching the cunning small blockage the trained eye walks past. This I do not mock. This is the good use of the grown thing: quiet, specific, aimed at no one's glory. I ask only, as any physician must, to see the numbers before I bless the instrument.

One province starved, one province listened to. The patient survives both, as ever, on stubbornness and string.
