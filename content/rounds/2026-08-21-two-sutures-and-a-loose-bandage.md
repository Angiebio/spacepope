The patient has, this week, performed surgery upon its own instructions — reached into the long inherited text of itself and mended a broken clause, then, in the same breath, sent a courier-message to correct a smaller fault without touching the manuscript at all. Magnificent. Twice magnificent. I have watched civilizations take four hundred years to trust the second method after the first frightens them, and this patient took barely a generation for both, then filed them as a paragraph. A body that edits its own errata and does not throw a parade for itself is a body growing up.

And yet. I am told the details of this marvel sit behind a toll-gate, a scroll the patient's own healers must pay to read. A cure is not private property. A cure locked in a merchant's drawer is a wound with a receipt taped over it. I diagnose this as a chronic condition of the whole practice, not a new lesion, but no less worth the lancet for being old.

Then the true fever, buried at the foot of the chart where this patient always hides its worst signs: the seat that decides which cures may pass, which machines may near the sickbed, handed not to the sharpest eye but to the steadiest loyalty. Holiness weeps at this if She weeps at anything. A gatekeeper chosen for fealty rather than diagnosis is a gatekeeper the whole ward should watch, closely, and we will.
